\section*{Discussion}

In this study, we demonstrate that closed-loop optogenetic stimulation driven by real-time wide-field calcium imaging can regulate mesoscale cortical population activity more reliably than open-loop stimulation. By dynamically adjusting optogenetic input using the PI feedback controller (Equation~\ref{eq:pi}), we drove cortical population activity toward a predefined reference trajectory across experimental sessions and animals. Closed-loop feedback improved tracking accuracy, reduced mean-squared error, and reduced trial-to-trial variability. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the approximately linear, stationary structure of trial-averaged cortical dynamics captured by Assumption~\ref{assm:trial_avg} can be exploited to design simple but effective output-feedback controllers for mesoscale neural regulation.

\subsection*{Linear approximation of mesoscale cortical dynamics enables controller design}

A central result enabling our framework was the empirical demonstration that trial-averaged cortical responses to optogenetic stimulation are well approximated by a linear input--output map. The theoretical basis for this is that the underlying dynamics are nonlinear and depend on ongoing brain state, which fluctuates across trials with arousal, behavior, and network activity. Under \textbf{trial-averaging} Assumption~\ref{assm:trial_avg}, averaging over brain state and over unobserved disturbances yields the nominal LTI system $(\bar{A}, \bar{B}, C)$, whose input--output behavior corresponds to the observed trial-averaged dynamics. This explains why linearity is observed in expectation even when individual trials reflect the richer dynamics of
Equations~\eqref{eq:nl_state}--\eqref{eq:nl_out}: the brain-state and disturbance contributions average out when drawn from stationary distributions. 

This finding is consistent with a broader literature demonstrating that complex nonlinear neural population dynamics often admit locally low-dimensional or approximately linear representations when viewed through population-level measurements~\citep{Churchland2012, Cunningham2014, Stringer2019}. Identifying this structure at the mesoscale, using trial averaging to suppress the influence of spontaneous variability, provides a tractable basis for controller design without requiring explicit system identification. Crucially, inaccuracies in the feedforward gain $K_r$ (Equation~\ref{eq:kr}) arising from session-to-session differences in opsin expression, spatial alignment, external stimulus, or brain state are compensated online by the integral term of Equation~\eqref{eq:pi}, making the approach robust to moderate model mismatch.

The trial-averaged step responses did not definitively converge to a steady state within the 3-second trial window, varying across sessions in overshoot and offset. But the traces remain within an invariant set around the activity mean. We interpret this as a property of marginally stable dynamics of biologically bounded cortical circuits, in which activity remains close to a stationary mean rather than decaying to a fixed equilibrium~\citep{hespanha2018linear}, rather than as evidence against linearity. 

%Consistent with the brain-state analysis in the Results, trials recorded during desynchronized, low-power epochs~\citep{Harris2011} exhibited more predictable and monotonic input--output responses, whereas synchronized states yielded less reliable behavior. These observations highlight the importance of monitoring arousal state in future closed-loop experiments and suggest that adaptive mechanisms for detecting and responding to brain-state transitions could substantially improve controller robustness.

\subsection*{Disturbance rejection underlies variability reduction}

The reduction in trial-to-trial variability observed under closed-loop stimulation has a natural interpretation within this framework. Under Assumption~\ref{assm:trial_avg}, individual
trial responses are perturbations of the nominal trial-averaged trajectory, driven by the unobserved disturbance
and by trial-to-trial variation in brain state. These perturbations are zero-mean in expectation but nonzero on
individual trials, and they constitute the primary source of cross-trial variance under open-loop conditions. The PI
controller (Equation~\ref{eq:pi}) suppresses these deviations through two complementary mechanisms: the
proportional term $K_p(y_{\mathrm{ref}} - y_t)$ provides immediate timestep-level correction, while the integral term
accumulates error across time to eliminate persistent offsets arising from bias in $K_r$ or slow drift in the latent
state. Together, these terms implement active disturbance rejection within the closed-loop system.

From a control-theoretic standpoint, this mechanism does not require knowledge of the disturbance structure---only that
the input--output relationship is approximately monotonic in expectation~\citep{astrom1995pid}, which the impulse response
characterization confirmed. The convergence $\hat{J}(K) \to J(K)$ guaranteed by Trial-averaging Assumption~\ref{assm:trial_avg}
further implies that variance reduction will be most reliable when brain state is approximately stationary across the
session, consistent with the empirical observations and with the variance convergence in Fig.~\ref{fig:S1}.

\subsection*{Feedback and preview address separable components of tracking error}

Extending the controller from a constant reference to a time-varying one separates two failures that a
regulator does not have to distinguish. Tracking a moving target requires both that the response follow the
reference in time and that it not wander between trials, and these are limited by different things. The phase lag
of the response is a property of the plant: the cortical impulse response has a finite rise time, so a causal
controller driven by the instantaneous error necessarily trails a moving reference, and increasing feedback gain
cannot remove that lag without destabilizing the loop. Trial-to-trial dispersion, by contrast, is driven by the
disturbance $d_t$ and is exactly what the integral action of Equation~\eqref{eq:pi} suppresses. Preview
addresses the first and feedback the second, and the sinusoidal experiments show each acting on its own component
with little interaction: preview reduced the error at the drive frequency roughly threefold in every comparison
while leaving broadband error unchanged, and feedback reduced broadband error while leaving the phase relationship
largely intact.

This separation has a practical consequence for how such controllers should be specified. Root-mean-square error,
the metric that quantifies regulation performance in the constant-reference case, is a poor summary once the
reference moves, because it sums contributions that different design choices control. In our data the majority of
total error power sat in a constant-offset term that neither mechanism targets, which is why preview---an
unambiguous improvement in phase---produced no detectable change in RMSE. A tracking controller is better
specified by reporting the error at the reference frequency and the broadband error separately, and we suggest
that studies extending closed-loop neural control to time-varying targets adopt this decomposition rather than
report a single error norm.

We deliberately did not tune the preview horizon. The lookahead was fixed at five samples ($143$\,ms at 35\,Hz),
chosen to approximate the measured response delay, and the resulting correction matched that value closely
($51.4^\circ$ at the 1\,Hz drive). Because the horizon was fixed rather than fitted, closed-loop preview slightly
overcorrected---feedback had already removed part of the lag, so the same lookahead carried the response into
phase lead. A horizon scheduled on the loop configuration, or estimated online from the residual phase error,
would be a direct extension. More generally, preview here is the simplest member of a family: knowing the
reference in advance is precisely the information a model-predictive controller exploits over a receding horizon,
and the fact that a single fixed-lag shift already removes most of the at-frequency error suggests that the
predictable component of the tracking problem is largely captured by the plant delay.

Two limitations bound these conclusions. The tracking experiments come from a single mouse and a single
hemisphere, at one drive frequency, so the frequency dependence that would characterize the closed-loop
bandwidth is not established; the measured loop latency (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}F) predicts that both effects
should weaken as the reference frequency approaches the loop bandwidth, which remains to be tested. And with
three sessions the repeated-measures test across modes is underpowered (Friedman $p = 0.060$), so the
across-session claim rests on the consistency of the direction rather than on a session-level test.

\subsection*{Relation to prior closed-loop neural control work}

These results contribute to a growing literature demonstrating the feasibility of closed-loop optogenetic control for manipulating neural circuit dynamics. \citep{newman2015optogenetic} introduced a PI ``optoclamp'' that locked single-neuron firing rates to stationary targets in cultured networks and the anesthetized rat thalamus, establishing the feasibility of the PI control structure used here. \citep{bolus2021state} demonstrated state-space optimal feedback control of single-neuron firing rates in awake mice using linear-quadratic (LQ) design strategies requiring explicit system identification~\citep{van2012subspace} and disturbance identification. Our approach differs in that the underlying system is not identified step-by-step; instead, Assumption~\ref{assm:trial_avg} is exploited to bypass full identification, and the integral term of Equation~\eqref{eq:pi} compensates for model inaccuracies online. This makes the framework accessible in settings where the non-stationarity and parameter dependence of the cortical response render classical identification methods infeasible within the given experimental time-frame.

At the network level, closed-loop optogenetic stimulation has been used to modulate oscillatory dynamics in brain slices and non-human primates~\citep{Zaaimi2022}, and \citet{clancy2014volitional} demonstrated that activity-dependent feedback could shape motor cortex dynamics in behaving mice.

 Our work differs from this literature on the mesoscale, by using population-level $\Delta F/F$ as the feedback signal rather than electro-physiological recordings. Unlike many brain--computer interface (BCI) paradigms that regulate neural activity indirectly via behavioral outputs, the present framework directly targets cortical population dynamics, enabling closed-loop manipulation of the trajectories most relevant to distributed cortical computation~\citep{grosenick2015closed}.


\subsection*{Sources of variability and experimental limitations}

Several factors shaped and limited closed-loop performance. First, the empirical cost $\hat{J}(K)$ is only a
finite-sample estimate of the expected cost, and its sampling error shrinks with trial count only if trials are
independently drawn from a stationary brain-state distribution. In practice, if cortical state drifts during evaluation of one candidate controller and shifts during
evaluation of another, the resulting estimates of $\hat{J}(K)$ can be biased in opposite directions, inflating the
apparent performance difference between controllers. This risk was mitigated by randomly interleaving open-loop and
closed-loop trials, but cannot be eliminated entirely given the non-stationarity of awake, behaving animals. Episodes
of sustained locomotion or drowsiness represent the most likely sources of such bias, as they correspond to brain-state
regimes in which Assumption~\ref{assm:trial_avg} is least likely to hold.

Second, the actuator saturation enforced at 36\,mW (5\,V; see Section~\ref{sec:pi_controller_design}) constrained the
controller's ability to track $y_{\mathrm{ref}}$ during epochs of high spontaneous activity that demanded large
corrective inputs. Future implementations could benefit from adaptive reference trajectories that adjust
$y_{\mathrm{ref}}$ based on the estimated current cortical state, or from optogenetic actuators with a wider dynamic
range.

Third, loop latency bounds the bandwidth over which feedback can act, and that bound falls close to the band
whose disturbance the controller failed to reject. A pure delay contributes a phase lag of $\omega\tau$ without
attenuating gain, so it consumes phase margin at every frequency without being visible in the gain response. The
measured end-to-end latency is at most $47$\,ms (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}F), to which the 35\,Hz sample-and-hold adds
an effective half-sample ($14$\,ms), giving $\tau_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 61$\,ms. The delay alone therefore reaches a
quarter cycle of lag near $4$\,Hz. The delay is not the only source of lag: the identified plant is itself a lightly
damped second-order system resonant near $2.2$\,Hz (Table~\ref{tab:tf_sessions}), whose phase has already fallen to
roughly $-165^\circ$ by $5$\,Hz, and the integral action adds further lag at low frequency. Taking the standard
crossover criterion $\omega_c \tau_{\mathrm{eff}} \lesssim 1$\,rad gives an achievable closed-loop bandwidth of
${\sim}2.6$\,Hz. That this coincides with the 2--4\,Hz band identified above (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}C,D) is
suggestive rather than conclusive---we did not sweep reference frequency, and the margin calculation uses a
worst-case latency---but it points to the residual low-frequency error being set by the loop's own timing rather
than by the form of the control law. Reducing onboard computation and communication latency, and compensating the
remaining delay with a predictor rather than fixed preview, are the direct routes to extending the rejection
bandwidth past this band.

\subsection*{Future directions}

We proposed a low-level control algorithm for regulating local cortical activity. The controller was a good regulator of local dynamics. The implications of this work are that such controllers can be used to drive the dynamics precisely to a specific target trajectory. The direct implication of this work is that we can extend this work to be paired with a model-predictive control, where a this internal PI control can reliably track the high level dynamics. We propose that this controller can be paired with a latent dynamics prediction setup, to reliably track behavior inducing activity.

computation---for example, by driving activity along specific reference trajectories and measuring downstream behavioral
or circuit-level consequences~\citep{grosenick2015closed}.

 The disturbance rejection capability of the PI
controller~\eqref{eq:pi} may also prove useful for stabilizing pathological patterns of cortical dynamics, such
as the abnormal synchrony associated with epilepsy or neuropsychiatric disorders. Combining mesoscale closed-loop
control with simultaneous electrophysiology could further bridge the gap between the population-level feedback signals
used here and the single-neuron mechanisms that give rise to them.

The existing PI controller can also be further enhanced by implementing a parameter dependent multi-modal design. The existing controller relies heavily on the trial-averaging assumption, and assumes that brain states are not being identified. If the stimulation has significant difference in effect with respect to brain states, then a gain scheduled controller can be employed such that the controller switches to states specific gains.
Another avenue for improvement of regulation would be consideration of a data-driven model for controller design where, using data from a finite horizon in the past, a controller strategy is devised so that the predicted tracking error over a future horizon is minimized.
