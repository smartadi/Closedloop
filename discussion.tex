\section*{Discussion}

In this study we show that closed-loop optogenetic stimulation, driven by real-time wide-field calcium imaging, regulates mesoscale cortical activity more reliably than open-loop stimulation. A PI feedback controller (Equation~\ref{eq:pi}) drove cortical activity toward a predefined reference across sessions and animals, and reduced both tracking error (RMSE) and trial-to-trial variability. The error left under feedback depended on brain state, and the loop partially rejected a disturbance predicted from the opposite hemisphere. With a moving reference, feedback and preview corrected separate parts of the tracking error.

\subsection*{Linear approximation of mesoscale cortical dynamics enables controller design}

A central result of this work is that trial-averaged cortical responses to optogenetic stimulation are well approximated by a linear input--output map. The underlying dynamics are nonlinear and depend on brain state, which fluctuates across trials. Under Assumption~\ref{assm:trial_avg}, the brain-state and disturbance contributions in Equations~\eqref{eq:nl_state}--\eqref{eq:nl_out} average out when they are drawn from stationary distributions, which leaves the nominal LTI system $(\bar{A}, \bar{B}, C)$. Linearity therefore holds in expectation even when single trials reflect richer dynamics, and this gives a tractable basis for controller design.

We kept more than two poles, although a two-pole model predicted held-out trials nearly as well (Results), because the higher-order fits separate a fast damped oscillation, whose time constant was similar in three of four sessions (${\approx}0.11$\,s), from slower modes that differ between sessions. A controller acting within a trial has to anticipate the shared fast dip, while the session-specific slower dynamics are consistent with tuning the gains per session.


%Consistent with the brain-state analysis in the Results, trials recorded during desynchronized, low-power epochs~\citep{Harris2011} exhibited more predictable and monotonic input--output responses, whereas synchronized states yielded less reliable behavior. These observations highlight the importance of monitoring arousal state in future closed-loop experiments and suggest that adaptive mechanisms for detecting and responding to brain-state transitions could substantially improve controller robustness.

\subsection*{Disturbance rejection underlies variability reduction}

The same framework explains the reduction in trial-to-trial variability under closed loop. Under Assumption~\ref{assm:trial_avg}, single-trial responses are perturbations of the nominal trajectory, driven by the unobserved disturbance and by brain state. They are zero-mean in expectation but nonzero on individual trials, and they are the main source of cross-trial variance in open loop. The PI controller (Equation~\ref{eq:pi}) suppresses them in two ways. The proportional term $K_p e_t$ corrects at every time step. The integral term removes persistent offsets, whether from slow drift in the latent state or from session-to-session errors in the feedforward gain $K_r$ (Equation~\ref{eq:kr}). The impulse experiments already point to this picture: the scatter of single responses around the trial average grew or shrank with brain state much as ongoing activity did in a stimulus-free window (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}G), consistent with state acting as a disturbance added to a fixed response rather than as a change in the response itself.

The state dependence in Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}D can be understood through the internal model principle~\citep{francis1976internal}: a feedback loop regulates its output against a class of disturbances only to the extent that it contains a model of the signals generating them. A PI controller contains exactly one such model, a constant, through its integrator, and its proportional term adds broadband but finite rejection up to the loop bandwidth. Regularizability then asks, state by state, whether the disturbance that comes with that state falls within what the loop models. Movement-related disturbance does: open-loop error rose with motion ($p = 0.013$, not below the corrected threshold, but confirmed within sessions, $p = 0.0020$) while closed-loop error did not ($p = 0.27$). The 2--4\,Hz fluctuation does not. It left the open loop unchanged ($p = 0.76$) but degraded closed-loop regulation ($p = 0.044$, not below the corrected threshold), consistent with a disturbance whose frequency content lies near or beyond the bandwidth that the loop latency allows (see below), and which a constant-plus-proportional model cannot represent. The trend in the open-minus-closed-loop gap, the usual summary of how much feedback helps, misses this distinction. The gap widened with motion (by $0.23\,\%\,\Delta F/F$ per SD, $[0.09, 0.37]$; $t(10) = 3.57$, $p = 0.0051$) and did not change significantly with any other state, including relative 2--4\,Hz power ($p = 0.081$), absolute 1--4\,Hz power ($p = 0.31$) and the initial deviation ($p = 0.69$). A widening gap can come from a rising open loop as much as from a holding closed loop, so we do not use the gap trend to judge what the controller can regulate. This reading makes a testable prediction: an internal model of the low-frequency fluctuation, for example a resonant mode at the dominant 2--4\,Hz frequency in place of the plain integrator, should restore regulation in this band.

\subsection*{Feedback and preview address separable components of tracking error}

Moving from a constant to a time-varying reference separates two failures that a regulator does not need to tell apart. The first is lag, a property of the plant: the cortical impulse response has a finite rise time, so a causal controller driven by the instantaneous error must trail a moving reference, and raising the feedback gain cannot remove that lag without destabilizing the loop. The second is trial-to-trial dispersion, driven by the disturbance $d_t$, which feedback suppresses. Preview corrected the first and feedback the second, with little interaction.

This affects how such controllers should be evaluated. RMSE summarizes regulation to a constant reference well, but once the reference moves it adds together contributions that different design choices control. In our data the largest single term, 44 to 55\% of the total error power, was a constant offset, which is why preview, a clear improvement in phase, produced no detectable change in RMSE. We suggest that studies extending closed-loop neural control to time-varying targets report the error at the reference frequency and the broadband error separately, instead of a single error norm.

We did not tune the preview horizon. The lookahead was fixed at five samples to approximate the measured response delay. Because feedback had already removed part of the lag, the same lookahead slightly overcorrected under closed loop and pushed the response into phase lead. A horizon set according to the loop configuration, or estimated online from the residual phase error, would be a direct extension. The tracking experiments also come from a single mouse and hemisphere at one drive frequency, and three sessions are too few for significance tests.

\subsection*{Relation to prior closed-loop neural control work}

\citet{newman2015optogenetic} introduced a PI ``optoclamp'' that locked single-neuron firing rates to fixed targets in cultured networks and the anesthetized rat thalamus, establishing the PI control structure used here. \citet{bolus2021state} demonstrated state-space optimal feedback control of single-neuron firing rates in awake mice, using linear-quadratic (LQ) designs that required explicit system identification~\citep{van2012subspace} and disturbance identification. We instead design against the trial-averaged system of Assumption~\ref{assm:trial_avg}, which needs a low-order model of the mean impulse response but no model of the disturbance or of single-trial dynamics. This makes the framework usable where the non-stationarity and parameter dependence of the cortical response make classical identification infeasible within the time of an experiment. Our work also differs from this literature in working at the mesoscale, with population-level $\Delta F/F$ as the feedback signal instead of electrophysiological recordings, and in acting on cortical population dynamics directly~\citep{grosenick2015closed} instead of through behavior, as in neurofeedback paradigms~\citep{clancy2014volitional}.

\subsection*{Sources of variability and experimental limitations}

Five factors limited closed-loop performance or its measurement. First, the empirical cost $\hat{J}(K)$ is a finite-sample estimate of the expected cost, and its sampling error shrinks with trial count only if trials are drawn independently from a stationary brain-state distribution. If cortical state drifts between the evaluation of two controllers, their estimates of $\hat{J}(K)$ can be biased in opposite directions and inflate the apparent difference between them. Randomly interleaving open-loop and closed-loop trials reduces this risk but cannot remove it in awake, behaving animals. Sustained locomotion and drowsiness are the most likely sources of such bias, because Assumption~\ref{assm:trial_avg} is least likely to hold in these regimes.

Second, the laser's power ceiling (Materials and Methods, \nameref{sec:pi_controller_design}), about 1\,mW at the focal plane in most 2025 sessions and 1.6\,mW in the first two, limited the controller's ability to track $y_{\mathrm{ref}}$ during epochs of high spontaneous activity that needed large corrective inputs. Commands reached it in seven of the 13 sessions recorded in 2025. A reference that adapts to the estimated cortical state, or a higher power ceiling, could help.

Third, loop latency bounds the bandwidth over which feedback can act. A pure delay contributes a phase lag of $\omega\tau$ without attenuating gain, so it consumes phase margin at every frequency. The measured end-to-end latency is at most $47$\,ms (Figure~\ref{fig:figure1}F), to which the 35\,Hz sample-and-hold adds an effective half-sample ($14$\,ms), giving $\tau_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 61$\,ms. The delay alone therefore reaches a quarter cycle of lag near $4$\,Hz, and the standard crossover criterion $\omega_c \tau_{\mathrm{eff}} \lesssim 1$\,rad gives an achievable closed-loop bandwidth of ${\sim}2.6$\,Hz. This coincidence with the 2--4\,Hz band that the controller failed to regulate (Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}D and Table~\ref{tab:fig4_state_stats}) is suggestive but not conclusive, since we did not sweep the reference frequency and the margin calculation uses a worst-case latency. It suggests that loop timing limits rejection in this band, so a resonant internal model (see above) would need a shorter loop delay, or a predictor that compensates it (see Future directions), to act there. It also predicts that feedback should lose effect as the reference frequency approaches this bandwidth. Reducing onboard computation and communication latency is one direct route to extending the rejection bandwidth past this band.

Fourth, the controller fed back on the blue channel alone, without hemodynamic correction, because correction needs the interleaved violet frame and cannot be applied within one control interval. The closed-loop analyses (Figures~\ref{fig:figure3}--\ref{fig:figure5}) use that same uncorrected signal, so that they describe what the loop actually regulated. Hemodynamic fluctuations overlap the 2--4\,Hz band, so part of what we call low-frequency power in Figure~\ref{fig:figure4} may be vascular rather than neuronal, and the loop corrects both alike. The impulse analysis of Figure~\ref{fig:figure2} uses the hemodynamically corrected signal, and there absolute 2--4\,Hz power still raised trial-to-trial variability in every session (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}G). That at least part of the band-power effect is neuronal is therefore supported, but we cannot apportion the closed-loop effect between the two sources. Online hemodynamic correction, or a red-shifted calcium indicator, would remove the ambiguity.

Fifth, the scope of the data is limited. The impulse characterization rests on four sessions from three mice, and its state analysis on per-session slopes with three degrees of freedom. We used inhibitory stimulation of one region of somatosensory cortex, so whether the same linear description and controller carry over to excitation, to other areas, or to larger perturbations is untested. The Local component of Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}F is defined as a residual, the activity the contralateral predictor does not explain, and so inherits any error of that predictor. The time-varying reference experiments (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}) cover three sessions, and we report their direction and consistency rather than significance.

\subsection*{Future directions}

The PI controller is a low-level regulator of local cortical activity. It could serve as the inner loop of a larger design, tracking trajectories set by a higher-level model-predictive controller or by a latent-dynamics model of the activity patterns that drive behavior. Because it relies on the trial-averaging assumption and does not identify brain state, a gain-scheduled version that switches to state-specific gains could help where stimulation effects differ substantially between brain states.

A model-predictive controller (MPC) that predicts cortical activity and plans the laser over the next second improved on PI in simulation (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}). We modelled the controlled site jointly with 100 sites across dorsal cortex and the laser command as a linear vector autoregressive process. On held-out open-loop trials this model predicted the controlled signal 200\,ms ahead with $R^2 = 0.42$, against $0.10$ for the last observed value and $0.02$ for a univariate autoregressive model of the kind benchmarked on spontaneous widefield activity~\citep{lu2025benchmarking} (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}G). Used as the MPC's predictor over a 1\,s horizon, it lowered the settled-window RMSE from a median of $1.46$ to $0.82\%\,\Delta F/F$ relative to the PI controller as it actually ran, on all 108 trials of the session, and cut the variance across trials over the same window by about 40\% (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}C--F). Given the future of the simulated cortex, the same controller reached $0.27\%$, so most of the error that remains under MPC comes from activity it could not predict from the past. These figures are an upper bound: the simulated cortex is linear, shares its model family with the controller, responds to the laser identically in every brain state, and comes from a single session. They indicate nonetheless that part of the error left by the PI loop is predictable from ongoing cortical activity, and that prediction, rather than feedback alone, is a route to removing it.
