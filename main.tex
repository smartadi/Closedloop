Mesoscale brain dynamics have been linked to a wide range of perceptual, cognitive and motor functions. However, establishing causal links between mesoscale dynamics and behavior remains challenging, because it requires not only perturbing these dynamics but also precisely shaping them. Here we show that closed-loop optogenetic stimulation can steer cortical activity in real time, using feedback from the ongoing activity it controls.
We first find that the trial-averaged responses of a cortical region to optogenetic stimulation are approximately linear and time-invariant. On this basis, we design a linear output-feedback controller that tracks a reference level of activity.
The controller holds local cortical activity at the reference more accurately than open-loop inhibitory stimulation in all 15 sessions from 4 mice, and reduces trial-to-trial variability, consistent with disturbance rejection.
Controller performance depends on brain state: relative to open loop, it improves with movement and degrades with slow-wave-like delta-band (1--4\,Hz) activity.
Predicting the disturbance on each trial from the opposite hemisphere, we show that feedback partially rejects it.
With a time-varying reference, previewing the upcoming reference cancels the phase lag of the cortical response, whereas feedback reduces error and variability.
Finally, in a replay of recorded trials, a model-predictive controller built on a cortical forecasting model lowers the error left by feedback by about 40\%.
Together, these results show that approximately linear, time-invariant trial-averaged dynamics can be used to model and control regional cortical activity, and that the error left under control is informative about brain state.
\end{abstract}


\section*{Significance Statement}
We introduce a closed-loop control framework that steers cortical activity toward a desired reference. Using wide-field calcium imaging, we read out regional cortical activity in real time and use it to set targeted optogenetic stimulation of that region. Closed-loop stimulation tracks the reference more accurately than open-loop stimulation and reduces trial-to-trial variability across sessions and animals. The error that remains under feedback depends on cortical state around the time of stimulation, and feedforward preview corrects the phase lag when tracking time-varying references. Finally, spontaneous activity in the controlled region is well predicted by a linear model of the opposite hemisphere, which lets us measure the disturbance the controller rejects on each trial.
