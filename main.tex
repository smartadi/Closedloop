\begin{abstract}
Dynamics of brain activity at the mesoscopic scale have been implicated in a host of perceptual, cognitive, and motor phenomena. However, establishing causal links between mesoscale dynamics and behavior remains challenging. We show that closed-loop optogenetic stimulation can steer cortical activity in real time against ongoing neural dynamics.
We first find that the trial-averaged responses of a cortical region to optogenetic stimulation are approximately linear and time-invariant. On this basis we design a linear output-feedback controller to track a desired reference level. The controller outperforms open-loop stimulation at reference tracking in all 15 sessions and reduces trial-to-trial variability in 14 of 15 sessions, consistent with disturbance rejection. The error that remains under feedback is state-dependent: it tracks the initial deviation from the reference early in the trial and ongoing low-frequency power later. Feedback keeps the output regulated as movement increases, but not as 2--4\,Hz power increases. Predicting the disturbance on each trial from the opposite hemisphere, we show that the closed loop partially rejects it. With a time-varying reference we separate feedback from feedforward preview: feedback reduces error and variability, whereas preview, model-based information added to the controller, cancels the phase lag of the cortical response, so the two correct distinct components of the tracking error. Together, these results show that stationary, linear trial-averaged dynamics can be used to model and control regional cortical activity, and that the error left under control is informative about brain state.
\end{abstract}


\section*{Significance Statement}
We introduce a closed-loop control framework that steers cortical activity toward a desired reference. Using wide-field calcium imaging, we read out regional cortical activity in real time and use it to set targeted optogenetic stimulation of that region. Closed-loop stimulation tracks the reference more accurately than open-loop stimulation and reduces trial-to-trial variability across sessions and animals. The error that remains under feedback depends on cortical state around the time of stimulation, and feedforward preview corrects the phase lag when tracking time-varying references. Finally, spontaneous activity in the controlled region is well predicted by a linear model of the opposite hemisphere, which lets us measure the disturbance the controller rejects on each trial.
