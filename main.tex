\begin{abstract}
Dynamics of brain activity at the mesoscopic scale have been implicated in a host of perceptual, cognitive, and motor phenomena. However, establishing causal links between mesoscale dynamics and behavioral outcomes remains challenging.  We show that closed-loop optogenetic manipulation can steer cortical activity in real time from ongoing neural dynamics.
We observe that the trial-averaged responses of a cortical region to optogenetic stimulation exhibit approximately linear, time-invariant behavior. For this setting, we design a linear output-feedback controller to track a desired reference state. This controller outperforms open-loop stimulation at reference tracking across all sessions for trial-averaged responses, and trial-to-trial variability is reduced under closed-loop control across all sessions, demonstrating a qualitative disturbance rejection phenomenon. The error that remained under feedback was state-dependent: it tracked the cortical state at stimulus onset, chiefly the initial deviation from the reference early and ongoing 2--4\,Hz power later. Furthermore, motion affected trials were observed to be more steerable. The disturbance was predictable from the opposite hemisphere, using known disturbance we were able to quantify that closed loop actively works on disturbance rejection. With a time-varying reference we separated feedback from feedforward preview: feedback reduced error and variability, whereas preview, a model based information added to the controller, canceled the phase lag of the cortical response, the two correcting distinct components of the tracking error. Collectively, these results establish that stationary and linear averaged-dynamics can be used for modeling and controlling regional cortical activity and be informative of brain state effects.  
\end{abstract}


\section*{Significance Statement}
Our study introduces a closed-loop control framework for manipulating cortical activity toward a desired reference trajectory. Using wide-field calcium imaging, we extract regional cortical activity in real time and use this information to drive targeted optogenetic stimulation of neighboring areas. We demonstrate that closed-loop stimulation improves reference tracking performance in trial-averaged responses compared to open-loop stimulation. Moreover, trial-to-trial variability is significantly reduced under closed-loop control across multiple sessions and animals. Error remaining under feedback tracks cortical state at stimulus onset, and feedforward preview corrects the phase lag for time-varying references. Finally, we show that even during spontaneous, unstimulated activity, the underlying neuronal dynamics can be accurately approximated using linear models, supporting the feasibility of model-based closed-loop manipulation.
