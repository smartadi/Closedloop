\section*{Results}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
We built a closed-loop optogenetic system that precisely regulates the activity of a cortical region, monitored with wide-field calcium imaging in mice.
Evoked responses to stimulation scaled monotonically with stimulus amplitude, and a linear time-invariant model captured their dynamics well, which gave us an empirical basis for designing a controller.
We then show that this controller holds cortical activity at a target level and that how well it performs depends on brain state.
% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Experimental setup and closed-loop control architecture}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
We performed closed-loop experiments in head-fixed transgenic mice co-expressing GCaMP and an inhibitory opsin.
Each mouse sat in a behavioral chamber~\citep{Matveev2024} facing a blank screen, with its forelimbs resting on a freely moving wheel, and was not engaged in any task, so all recordings captured spontaneous activity.
We recorded cortical activity at 35\,Hz with wide-field calcium imaging (Figure~\ref{fig:figure1}A) and delivered spatially targeted optogenetic stimulation to a fixed cortical spot, which suppressed activity in the surrounding cortex (Figure~\ref{fig:spatial_spread}A,B).
We refer to the controlled signal as the output, or simply activity: the fractional change in fluorescence relative to its mean ($\Delta F/F$), averaged over a kernel of pixels adjacent to the stimulation spot (Figure~\ref{fig:figure1}B,C; see Materials and Methods).
In a subset of experiments, we also recorded video of the face and computed motion energy, the squared pixel change per unit time (see Materials and Methods).

We built an end-to-end system for real-time closed-loop control of mesoscale activity (Figure~\ref{fig:figure1}D).
In real time, software computes the error between the measured output and a reference $\Delta F/F$ value and computes the required optogenetic stimulus, which drives the cortical dynamics with a loop delay of at most 47\,ms and about half that on average (Figure~\ref{fig:figure1}E,F).
This delay limits the bandwidth over which feedback can act (see Discussion).

The controller combines a proportional--integral (PI) feedback term with open-loop (feedforward) compensation (Figure~\ref{fig:figure1}E).
The feedforward term supplies the nominal stimulus needed to reach the reference, scaled by a gain calibrated from open-loop stimulation experiments.
The PI term corrects the remaining error in real time as the brain state changes, compensating for an inaccurate feedforward gain, residual nonlinearities, and spontaneous trial-to-trial fluctuations.

\subsection*{Trial-averaged cortical dynamics are well approximated by a linear system}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
To characterize the input--output relationship, we delivered brief optogenetic impulses at discrete amplitudes, randomly interleaved across trials, in four sessions from three mice.
Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}A shows the trial-averaged responses to three of these amplitudes in one session.

The mean evoked suppression grew monotonically with stimulation amplitude: the stronger the stimulation, the deeper the trial-averaged suppression, even though single-trial responses varied (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}B; Materials and Methods).
This input--output relationship was approximately linear (slopes $-0.88$, $-1.32$, $-1.53$ and $-3.11\,\%\,\Delta F/F$ per mW; $R^2 = 0.97$, $0.73$, $0.88$ and $0.88$), which supports a linear feedforward controller calibrated from impulse-response data~\citep{astrom1995pid}.
To capture the response dynamics, we fit an LTI model to the impulse-response data of each session (Materials and Methods).
Models fit to the trial-averaged response of a random half of the trials predicted the trial-averaged response of the other half (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}C), with a pooled median held-out $R^2$ of $0.85$ across the four sessions; per-session medians ranged from $0.56$ in Mouse 5a (interquartile range $0.09$--$0.79$) to $0.95$ in Mouse 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}D).
The spread of held-out $R^2$ within each session reflects trial-to-trial variability in the response, which we examine below.
We allowed up to five poles and four zeros, selected the order once per session on all trials, and held it fixed when scoring held-out trials (Mouse 5a, three poles and one zero; Mice 5b and 1, four poles and three zeros; Mouse 3, five poles and four zeros).
A two-pole model predicted held-out trials almost as well (pooled held-out $R^2 = 0.81$ against $0.85$; $0.87$ against $0.85$ in Mouse 3).

A model fit in one session described the responses of other sessions only inconsistently (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}E): although the cross-session $R^2$ was moderate on average ($0.69$, against $0.99$ in sample), it ranged from $0.97$ for some session pairs to $0.12$ for others.
We therefore recalibrated the feedforward gain in every session rather than reusing a fitted model.
The partial similarity of the dynamics across sessions nonetheless made this calibration simple in practice: gains from earlier sessions served as a starting point and needed minor adjustments (Materials and Methods).

The fitted poles yield two timescales (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}F). The two modes overlap in time, so neither maps onto one feature of the response alone (in Mice 5b and 1 the slower mode contributes $60$--$68\%$ of the trough).
The dip timescale, the decay of the fastest mode (a damped 2.4--4.2\,Hz oscillation that shapes the initial suppression), was consistent across sessions (median $0.113$\,s; $0.106$--$0.114$\,s in Mice 5a, 5b and 1, and $0.14$\,s in Mouse 3; Materials and Methods), although it depends on model order: the one other model fitting almost as well ($R^2$ within $0.005$) gave $0.117$ instead of $0.106$\,s in Mouse 5a and $0.154$ instead of $0.114$\,s in Mouse 1.
The rebound timescale, the slowest mode, which governs how activity returns after the dip, varied across sessions ($0.27$\,s in Mouse 1, $0.50$\,s in Mouse 3 at the edge of the $0.5$\,s fit window, and beyond it in Mice 5a and 5b, where in Mouse 5a it adds a small sustained suppression, $6\%$ of the trough) and is correspondingly less well constrained: it exceeded the fit window in $39\%$ and $72\%$ of bootstrap refits in Mice 5a and 5b and in all refits in Mouse 3, against $3\%$ in Mouse 1 (Materials and Methods).
Accordingly, the peak-normalized responses share the dip time course and diverge only during the rebound, which was small relative to the dip (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}D).
The dip, consistent across sessions, therefore sets how quickly stimulation can move cortical activity, whereas the session-variable rebound acts on slower timescales, at which the integral term of the controller can compensate.

\subsection*{Single-trial variability reflects state-dependent ongoing activity}
The LTI model captured the trial-averaged response, but individual trials deviated from it, and the size of these deviations depended on brain state.
We characterized the state before each stimulus with three measures, all taken over the 1\,s before onset (Materials and Methods): motion energy from face video, a measure of the animal's movement; relative 2--4\,Hz ($\delta$) power, the fraction of 0.4--10\,Hz cortical power in the $\delta$ band, which reflects how strongly the spectrum is concentrated in slow rhythms; and absolute 2--4\,Hz power, which reflects the overall amplitude of slow fluctuations.
We quantified each trial's variability as the root-mean-square deviation of its response over the LTI fit window ($0$ to $0.5$\,s) from the trial-averaged response at its amplitude, which is the response the LTI model describes.
We normalized this variability within each session and binned trials by quartile of each state measure (1516 trials, four sessions; Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}G).
We computed the same measure over a stimulus-free window sampled between stimulus trials, to ask whether ongoing activity without stimulation shows the same state dependence (Materials and Methods).

In every session, single trials were least variable in the top motion quartile ($0.57$--$0.71$ times the session's typical trial), whereas the three lower quartiles stayed between $0.88$ and $1.30$, so the pattern was a drop at the highest motion rather than a graded trend (top versus bottom quartile, geometric mean over sessions $0.58$, range $0.50$--$0.81$; Figure~\ref{fig:state_quartile}A).
Variability rose from quartile to quartile with absolute $\delta$ power in all four sessions ($2.35$, range $1.70$--$2.71$), and showed no consistent pattern with relative $\delta$ power ($1.03$, range $0.83$--$1.16$).

Ongoing activity in the stimulus-free window followed the same pattern in each state (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}G, dashed line; top versus bottom quartile $0.60$, $1.08$ and $2.70$ for motion, relative and absolute $\delta$ power), and it deviated from its own trial average about as much as the evoked response did (ratio of within-session variabilities, $0.92$--$1.06$ across sessions).
Expressed relative to the stimulus-free window, response variability changed little across quartiles (top versus bottom quartile $0.98$, $0.95$ and $0.87$; Figure~\ref{fig:state_quartile}, second column); if anything, the response varied slightly less with absolute $\delta$ power than ongoing activity did.
These observations suggest that a fixed stimulus effect rides on ongoing activity whose amplitude depends on state, rather than that state changes how cortex responds to the stimulus (per-session values and supporting tests, Table~\ref{tab:stat_fig2g}).
On this reading, brain state acts as a disturbance added to an otherwise fixed plant, which is the situation feedback control is designed to handle, and it sets a floor on how well any fixed model can predict an individual trial; we test this directly in closed loop below (Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}).

The two $\delta$ measures are correlated but not redundant (Spearman $\rho = 0.47$ over the impulse trials).
We therefore carry both into the closed-loop analysis, relative power as a candidate state variable independent of overall power and absolute power as a measure of disturbance amplitude; there, absolute power is taken over 1--4\,Hz and all state measures are computed from $-2$ to $+3$\,s around onset (Materials and Methods).

\subsection*{Closed-loop control improves reference-tracking accuracy}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
We used the PI controller (Figure~\ref{fig:figure1}E; Materials and Methods) to hold spontaneous activity at $-5\%\,\Delta F/F$ for 3\,s per trial.
For each mouse, we first tuned the feedforward gain $K_r$ and the feedback gains $K_p$ and $K_i$ (Materials and Methods), and at the start of each session we adjusted them slightly to account for day-to-day changes.
We then ran a validation block with fixed gains, in which closed-loop trials and open-loop trials (feedforward only, no feedback) were randomly interleaved (Table~\ref{tab:gains}).
Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}A--D and F show one representative session (m10; $K_r = 0.1$, $K_p = 0.07$, $K_i = 0.1$), and Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}E and G--I pool all 15 sessions.
On single trials, the closed-loop controller adjusted the stimulation as activity changed, whereas the open-loop input was fixed regardless of what the cortex did (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}A).
As a result, trial-averaged closed-loop activity stayed close to the reference throughout stimulation, whereas open-loop activity carried the error of the feedforward calibration (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}B); the difference in mean input between conditions is the correction applied by feedback (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}C).

We quantified tracking accuracy on each trial as the root-mean-square error (RMSE) between activity and the reference.
In the representative session, closed loop lowered both the typical RMSE and its spread across trials (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}F).
To compare sessions on one scale, we took for each session the ratio of closed- to open-loop mean RMSE in each window, so that values below one mean closed loop tracked better, and tested the log ratio across sessions with a mixed model that allows for mouse (Materials and Methods).
Across all 15 sessions from 4 mice, closed loop lowered RMSE over the full 3\,s window in every session, by 23\% on average (geometric-mean ratio $0.77$; Table~\ref{tab:stat_fig3}).
The gap, averaged over sessions, opened at stimulation onset and persisted for the rest of the trial (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}G).
Because the initial deviation settles within the first second, we split the window at 1\,s, so that the 1--3\,s window mainly reflects the response to ongoing disturbances.
Closed loop lowered RMSE by 15\% in the first second (ratio $0.85$; $p = 2.0\times10^{-5}$) and by 32\% from 1 to 3\,s ($0.68$; $p = 1.1\times10^{-6}$), with the ratio below one in all 15 sessions in both windows (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}H).
Before and after stimulation the ratio stayed at one (geometric mean $1.01$ and $1.00$; $p = 0.84$ and $1.0$).

\subsection*{Closed-loop control reduces trial-to-trial variability}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Feedback also made responses more repeatable.
In the representative session, the variance across trials was the same in both conditions before stimulation (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}D).
During stimulation, open-loop variance fell only slightly, because a fixed input cannot respond to the deviations of individual trials, whereas closed-loop variance fell much further, because feedback acts on each trial's deviation as it develops.
Across all 15 sessions, the gap between conditions likewise opened at stimulation onset and closed after it (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}E).
In the per-session ratio of closed- to open-loop across-trial variance (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}I; same test as above), closed loop lowered variance by 35\% in the first second (ratio $0.65$; $p = 0.0039$) and by 46\% from 1 to 3\,s ($0.54$; $p = 5.4\times10^{-5}$), and left it unchanged before and after stimulation (geometric mean $1.04$ and $1.05$; $p = 0.98$ and $0.48$), so the reduction is confined to the period in which feedback is active.

The size of the improvement varied across sessions, likely reflecting differences in arousal, opsin expression and the overlap between the stimulated and recorded regions, but its direction did not: closed loop lowered RMSE in all 15 sessions and variance in 14 of 15 over the full window, and in at least 14 of 15 sessions in each sub-window.

\subsection*{Controller performance is brain-state dependent}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Feedback improved tracking on average, but tracking performance 
but how well it held activity on a given trial depended on brain state.
We characterized that state with four quantities: the initial deviation of activity from the reference, read at stimulus onset, and motion energy, relative 2--4\,Hz power and absolute 1--4\,Hz power, each measured from 2\,s before onset to the end of stimulation at $+3$\,s, because performance over a trial depends on activity both before and during stimulation (Materials and Methods).
Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}A shows closed-loop trials that are extreme in one factor and typical in the others, and Figure~\ref{fig:f4_exemplars}A shows such exemplars for every session.
Face video was available for 11 of the 15 sessions; the other 4 are excluded from all motion analyses.

We first asked how much of the per-trial tracking error (RMSE) each factor explains on its own, by fitting each session separately and computing the unique contribution of each factor in the first second and from 1 to 3\,s (Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}B,C; 11 sessions, 613 closed-loop trials; mean $\pm$ SEM across sessions).
Because the two power measures are correlated, each entered a separate model together with the initial deviation and motion.
The initial deviation explained most of the error in the first second but almost none later (unique $R^2 = 0.53 \pm 0.05$ and $0.03 \pm 0.03$), whereas absolute 1--4\,Hz power explained a similar share in both windows ($0.11 \pm 0.05$ and $0.11 \pm 0.06$).
Relative 2--4\,Hz power explained less ($0.05 \pm 0.03$ and $0.03 \pm 0.02$), and motion explained none (unique $R^2 \le 0.001$).
From 1 to 3\,s, absolute power was therefore the largest unique contributor, and it remained so when relative power was added to the same model ($0.07 \pm 0.04$; Figure~\ref{fig:f4_exemplars}B,C).

To see how each state affects open- and closed-loop error, we fit a mixed model to the 1--3\,s RMSE with condition, state and their interaction, and session and mouse as random effects (Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}D; 15 sessions from 4 mice and 1670 trials, or 11 sessions and 1190 trials for motion; Materials and Methods).
Slopes are in $\%\,\Delta F/F$ of RMSE per within-session standard deviation (SD) of the state.
We describe each state by two slopes.
The open-loop slope measures \emph{predictability}: a positive slope means that the state adds error that a fixed feedforward input cannot anticipate.
The closed-loop slope measures \emph{regularizability}: a flat slope means that feedback holds activity at the reference regardless of the state.
All $p$ values are uncorrected; Table~\ref{tab:fig4_state_stats} lists every estimate and marks those that survive Bonferroni correction.

Closed loop lowered error at every state, by $0.71$--$0.83\,\%\,\Delta F/F$ at the mean state (all $p \le 3.4\times10^{-5}$), and two states stood out.
Motion is a disturbance that the loop rejects.
Open-loop error rose with motion ($+0.16\,\%\,\Delta F/F$ per SD, $p = 0.013$; $n = 11$ sessions), but closed-loop error did not ($-0.07$, $p = 0.27$), and the open-loop slope exceeded the closed-loop slope in all 11 sessions.
Absolute 1--4\,Hz power is a disturbance that the loop reduces but cannot hold: it raised open-loop error ($+0.30$, $p = 6.6\times10^{-4}$; $n = 15$ sessions) and closed-loop error by as much ($+0.36$, $p = 1.6\times10^{-4}$).
The other two states had weaker effects.
The initial deviation raised both errors, like absolute power but less ($+0.18$ and $+0.15$; $p = 0.014$ and $0.028$).
Relative 2--4\,Hz power left open-loop error unchanged ($+0.02$, $p = 0.76$) and raised closed-loop error slightly ($+0.12$, $p = 0.044$), an effect that does not survive correction and that we do not build on.

Predictability as defined here differs from the trial-to-trial variability of Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}G, which measures how far single impulse responses scatter around their mean; for motion the two diverge, because impulse responses scatter less on movement trials, yet the reference is harder to hit in open loop.

A closed-loop error that rises with the state could still be a constant fraction of a growing open-loop error.
We therefore also measured \emph{attenuation}, the slope of the closed-to-open-loop error ratio against state (log scale).
The same two states separated.
With more motion, closed-loop error fell as a fraction of open-loop error, by 9\% per SD ($p = 0.0048$), whereas with more absolute 1--4\,Hz power it rose, by 8\% per SD ($p = 0.0014$).
Relative power raised the ratio slightly ($+5\%$, $p = 0.027$), and the initial deviation left it unchanged ($p = 0.89$), so the loop removes the same fraction of a large initial deviation as of a small one.
Slow, large-amplitude cortical fluctuations therefore carry the largest share of the error from 1 to 3\,s (Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}C), and they are the one state that the loop can neither hold nor attenuate as it grows.

\subsection*{Feedback rejects a state-dependent disturbance carried by the contralateral hemisphere}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
The two hemispheres are strongly co-active, so spontaneous activity in the stimulated (ipsilateral) region is well predicted from the opposite hemisphere. We fit a stimulation-blind linear predictor from contralateral pixels to the ipsilateral control readout on spontaneous, no-laser epochs (Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}E). It generalized to held-out spontaneous frames (contralateral$\rightarrow$ipsilateral $R^2 = 0.75$ in the representative session of Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}F; median $0.76$, range $0.44$--$0.98$, over the 13 sessions with a predictor), and so gives a trial-by-trial readout of the ongoing activity the controller has to contend with.

We used this predictor to separate two sources of activity on single trials. Applied to each stimulation trial, it gives the \emph{Global} component $G$, an estimate of the activity the ipsilateral region would have shown with no controller input. This is the disturbance the controller faces on that trial. Contralateral activity also changes during stimulation, so $G$ carries part of the laser response: in open loop its dip over 0--3\,s was $6$--$42\%$ of the readout's (median $23\%$), and we subtract that before using $G$ as the disturbance (Materials and Methods). The \emph{Local} component $L = A - G$ is what the laser and controller added on top of it (Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}F). This split is a construction, not a fitted model: it is what makes a per-trial disturbance measurable at all, since the predictor is fitted without reference to the laser. We therefore make no claim from the decomposition itself, and instead test the controller against the disturbance it defines.

We quantified how well closed-loop control rejects this disturbance with a per-trial energy ratio $\mathrm{RR} = \|A - r\|^2 / \|D\|^2$ over the steady-state window (1--3\,s, after the inhibitory onset transient). The numerator is the residual energy of the actual response about the reference ($r = -5\%\,\Delta F/F$). The denominator is the energy of the disturbance $D$, the contra-predicted excursion the controller must cancel, referenced to its own no-laser baseline and leak-corrected. Referencing the disturbance to its own baseline instead of to the target keeps the fixed setpoint offset from being counted as disturbance, so $\mathrm{RR} < 1$ means the controlled response carries less energy than the disturbance it counters.

A session enters this analysis only if a contralateral predictor can be fit for it, which is the case for thirteen of the fifteen (Materials and Methods). We kept eleven of these. In the other two the closed-loop trial average never reached the reference ($2.2$ and $1.4\%\,\Delta F/F$ above it at steady state), so a standing offset, not unrejected disturbance, made up most of their residual: it supplied $38\%$ and $59\%$ of the residual energy, against $17\%$ or less in every other session, and we excluded sessions in which it supplied more than one third (Materials and Methods).

Across these eleven sessions, closed-loop control rejected the disturbance: the geometric-mean ratio was $\mathrm{RR} = 0.58$ (mixed model with a session random intercept, one-sided $p = 1.3\times10^{-4}$; $n = 11$ sessions, 595 trials, 3 mice; Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}G; Table~\ref{tab:stat_fig4g}), and the median ratio was below one in every session. The evidence is at the level of sessions: the eleven come from three mice, one of which contributes a single session, too few for a mouse-level test (Materials and Methods).

Applying the same ratio to the open-loop trials of these sessions shows what feedback adds. The open loop left more energy about the reference than the disturbance it faced (median $\mathrm{RR} = 1.25$ against $0.72$ under closed loop, lower under feedback in all eleven sessions; one-sided $p = 4.9\times10^{-4}$). This paired comparison does not depend on how the disturbance is scaled, since any error in that scale divides out of both conditions. Most of the difference lies in where the response sits, not in how it fluctuates. The standing offset supplied $35\%$ of the open-loop residual against $7\%$ under closed loop (10 of 11 sessions, $p = 9.8\times10^{-4}$), because a fixed input sets the response level by its calibration and not by the reference, while the integral term removes that offset. Feedback also reduced the fluctuation itself, but by less (fluctuation energy fell from $0.66$ to $0.54$ of the disturbance, 10 of 11 sessions, $p = 0.0024$).

\subsection*{Feedforward control for variable reference tracking}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Feedback and preview correct different failures of sinusoidal tracking: feedback removes trial-to-trial variability, and preview removes the phase lag imposed by the plant. We set the controller to track a 1\,Hz sinusoidal reference for 4\,s and compared four modes: open loop (OL), open loop with preview (OL$+$p), closed loop (CL), and closed loop with preview (CL$+$p). The preview block shifts the reference forward by a fixed lookahead, set from the open-loop response, before it reaches the controller. This way the controller compensates for the delay of the cortical response and not only its amplitude (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}A). We report one representative session in detail (200 sinusoidal trials; $n = 51, 46, 46, 57$ trials for OL, OL$+$p, CL, CL$+$p) and the consistency of each effect across three sessions.

Open-loop tracking followed the reference in shape but lagged it and drifted between trials (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}B). The trial average retained the sinusoid in every mode, with the across-trial spread widest under open loop (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}C); the trial-average laser power that produced these responses is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}D.

Feedback reduced both the trial-to-trial spread and the error itself. Across-trial variance fell from $17.9$ to $14.8$ between OL and CL (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}E), and the time-resolved tracking error fell alongside it (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}F). Collapsing each trial to a single number, median RMSE fell from $3.82$ to $2.80$\,\%\,$\Delta F/F$ (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}G). Preview did not reproduce this effect: median RMSE changed little between OL and OL$+$p ($3.82$ versus $3.55$), and adding preview to closed-loop control did not improve it further (CL $2.80$ versus CL$+$p $3.30$). Closed-loop control with preview gave the tightest distribution of the four modes (SD $1.57$ versus $1.85$, $2.75$ and $2.19$ for OL, OL$+$p and CL) and reduced error relative to open loop (median $3.30$ versus $3.82$).

Preview instead corrected the lag. The cortical response lagged the reference by $60^\circ$ under open loop and by $36^\circ$ under closed loop; adding preview moved both to approximately zero ($5^\circ$ and $-9^\circ$ respectively; Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}H). The correction matched the lookahead by construction: a five-sample preview at 35\,Hz corresponds to $143$\,ms, or $51^\circ$ at the 1\,Hz drive frequency, and the measured open-loop correction across all pooled trials was $5.0$ samples ($51.4^\circ$, median).

All three effects held across sessions. Per-trial RMSE was lower under closed loop than open loop in 3/3 sessions (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}I), preview cancelled the lag in all three (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}J), and across-trial variance was lower under closed loop in 3/3 (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}K). Pooling per-trial RMSE across sessions showed the same reduction (mean $\pm$ SD: OL $4.58 \pm 1.77$, $n = 136$; CL $3.83 \pm 1.94$, $n = 100$) and no comparable change with preview (OL$+$p $4.47 \pm 2.22$, $n = 110$; CL$+$p $3.95 \pm 1.58$, $n = 132$). With three sessions we do not report significance tests; we treat the direction of each effect and its consistency across sessions as the result. The pattern is consistent with the error reduction coming from feedback and the lag correction from preview.

Decomposing the error shows why the two mechanisms do not overlap. Because the stimulation window contains exactly four cycles of the drive, per-trial squared error partitions without loss into a constant-offset term, a term at the drive frequency, and the remaining broadband term. Averaged over the three sessions, preview reduced the error at the drive frequency in both comparisons (OL $3.14 \rightarrow$ OL$+$p $0.89$, 3.5-fold; CL $1.78 \rightarrow$ CL$+$p $1.22$\,(\%\,$\Delta F/F)^2$, 1.5-fold), while feedback reduced the broadband term ($10.55 \rightarrow 6.62$ from OL to CL). The constant-offset term was the largest of the three in every mode ($10.97$, $13.13$, $10.22$ and $7.86$ for OL, OL$+$p, CL and CL$+$p). Preview therefore acts on the component of error that a fixed phase relationship can remove, and feedback on the component generated by ongoing spontaneous activity. Closed-loop control with preview was the only mode to engage both, and had the lowest total error power in two of three sessions.

\subsection*{Model-predictive control rejects disturbances in a replay of recorded trials}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
The PI controller corrects an error only after it has appeared. Part of the error it leaves is predictable, from ongoing activity across cortex and from the known effect of the laser, and a controller that plans ahead could act on it. We did not run such a controller on the rig, so we asked how one would have performed on the closed-loop trials we recorded, using a model fitted to the same session (one session of Mouse~1, 108 closed-loop trials; Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}). Everything in this section comes from simulation on recorded data and is descriptive.

We modelled the controlled site, 100 sites spread over dorsal cortex and the laser command as one linear vector autoregressive process with exogenous input (VARX). On held-out open-loop trials it predicted the controlled signal better than the last observed value and better than a univariate autoregressive model of the kind benchmarked on spontaneous widefield activity~\citep{lu2025benchmarking} (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}G). At every frame the model-predictive controller (MPC) used this model to predict the controlled signal over the next second for a candidate plan of laser commands, chose the plan that kept the prediction closest to the reference without abrupt changes in the command, applied its first command and planned again at the next frame (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}A,B).

To compare the two controllers on the same cortical activity, we drove the VARX fitted to the whole session with each trial's recorded one-step prediction residuals. Under the recorded laser this reproduces the recorded trial exactly, so the PI baseline is the trial as it ran on the rig. Under the MPC's laser, the residuals, the part of the activity that no model predicted, are replayed unchanged. The MPC's own model was fitted with that trial held out.

In this replay the MPC lowered the per-trial RMSE over the settled window (1--3\,s) from a median of $1.46$ under the recorded PI controller to $0.82\%\,\Delta F/F$, and was lower on all 108 trials (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}C--E). Across-trial variance over the same window fell by $39\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}F). The gain held across the range of command-smoothness penalties we tried (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}H). Given the future residuals, the same controller reached $0.27\%\,\Delta F/F$, so most of the error left under MPC is activity it could not predict from the past.

The replayed cortex shares the controller's model class, responds to the laser in the same way in every brain state, and comes from one session, so these values bound rather than measure what a live MPC would achieve. They do show that part of the error the PI loop leaves is predictable, and that a controller using that prediction removes it.

We repeated the replay on the sinusoidal session of Figure~\ref{fig:figure5} (Mouse~3), on the 57 trials run with feedback and preview (CL$+$p), the rig mode that combined both corrections. The 1\,Hz reference entered the MPC's cost over its prediction horizon, so the MPC saw the reference ahead of time, as the preview block does. The MPC lowered per-trial RMSE over the 4\,s window from a median of $3.00$ under the recorded CL$+$p controller to $2.70\%\,\Delta F/F$, and was lower on 56 of 57 trials (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}I,L). It kept the phase lag at zero, as preview had ($2^\circ$ under CL$+$p, $-1^\circ$ under MPC, against $71^\circ$ open loop; Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}K), and lowered across-trial variance to $0.68$ times that of CL$+$p (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}J). The gain was smaller than with the constant reference: even perfect prediction reached only $0.76$ times the CL$+$p error. Most of the remaining error (about two thirds of the squared error) fell in the half-cycles where the reference rose back toward zero. There the inhibitory laser was off for most of the time, because it can suppress activity but cannot drive the signal up. These values use the signal and reference timing the controller itself saw, so they differ slightly from those in Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}. They carry the same caveats as the constant-reference replay: one session, a simulated cortex of the controller's own model class, and a laser response that does not depend on brain state.
