\section*{Results}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
We built a closed-loop optogenetic system that precisely regulates the activity of a cortical region, monitored with wide-field calcium imaging in mice.
Evoked responses to stimulation scaled monotonically with stimulus amplitude, and a linear time-invariant model captured their dynamics well, which gave us an empirical basis for designing a controller.
We then show that this controller holds cortical activity at a target level and that how well it performs depends on brain state.
% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Experimental setup and closed-loop control architecture}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
We performed closed-loop experiments in head-fixed transgenic mice co-expressing GCaMP and an inhibitory opsin.
Each mouse sat in a behavioral chamber~\citep{Matveev2024} facing a blank screen, with its forelimbs resting on a freely moving wheel, and was not engaged in any task, so all recordings captured spontaneous activity.
We recorded cortical activity at 35\,Hz with wide-field calcium imaging (Figure~\ref{fig:figure1}A) and delivered spatially targeted optogenetic stimulation to a fixed cortical spot, which suppressed activity in the surrounding cortex (Figure~\ref{fig:spatial_spread}A,B).
We refer to the controlled signal as the output, or simply activity: the fractional change in fluorescence relative to its mean ($\Delta F/F$), averaged over a kernel of pixels adjacent to the stimulation spot (Figure~\ref{fig:figure1}B,C; see Materials and Methods).
In a subset of experiments, we also recorded video of the face and computed motion energy, the squared pixel change per unit time (see Materials and Methods).

We built an end-to-end system for real-time closed-loop control of mesoscale activity (Figure~\ref{fig:figure1}D).
In real time, software computes the error between the measured output and a reference $\Delta F/F$ value and computes the required optogenetic stimulus, which drives the cortical dynamics with a loop delay of at most 47\,ms and about half that on average (Figure~\ref{fig:figure1}E,F).
This delay limits the bandwidth over which feedback can act (see Discussion).

The controller combines a proportional--integral (PI) feedback term with open-loop (feedforward) compensation (Figure~\ref{fig:figure1}E).
The feedforward term supplies the nominal stimulus needed to reach the reference, scaled by a gain calibrated from open-loop stimulation experiments.
The PI term corrects the remaining error in real time as the brain state changes, compensating for an inaccurate feedforward gain, residual nonlinearities, and spontaneous trial-to-trial fluctuations.

\subsection*{Trial-averaged cortical dynamics are well approximated by a linear system}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
To characterize the input--output relationship, we delivered brief optogenetic impulses at discrete amplitudes, randomly interleaved across trials, in four sessions from three mice.
Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}A shows the trial-averaged responses to three of these amplitudes in one session.

The mean evoked suppression grew monotonically with stimulation amplitude: the stronger the stimulation, the deeper the trial-averaged suppression, even though single-trial responses varied (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}B; Materials and Methods).
This input--output relationship was approximately linear (slopes $-0.88$, $-1.32$, $-1.53$ and $-3.11\,\%\,\Delta F/F$ per mW; $R^2 = 0.97$, $0.73$, $0.88$ and $0.88$), which supports a linear feedforward controller calibrated from impulse-response data~\citep{astrom1995pid}.
To capture the response dynamics, we fit an LTI model to the impulse-response data of each session (Materials and Methods).
Models fit to the trial-averaged response of a random half of the trials predicted the trial-averaged response of the other half (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}C), with a pooled median held-out $R^2$ of $0.85$ across the four sessions; per-session medians ranged from $0.56$ in Mouse 5a (interquartile range $0.09$--$0.79$) to $0.95$ in Mouse 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}D).
The spread of held-out $R^2$ within each session reflects trial-to-trial variability in the response, which we examine below.
We allowed up to five poles and four zeros, selected the order once per session on all trials, and held it fixed when scoring held-out trials (Mouse 5a, three poles and one zero; Mice 5b and 1, four poles and three zeros; Mouse 3, five poles and four zeros).
A two-pole model predicted held-out trials almost as well (pooled held-out $R^2 = 0.81$ against $0.85$; $0.87$ against $0.85$ in Mouse 3).

A model fit in one session described the responses of other sessions only inconsistently (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}E): although the cross-session $R^2$ was moderate on average ($0.69$, against $0.99$ in sample), it ranged from $0.97$ for some session pairs to $0.12$ for others.
We therefore recalibrated the feedforward gain in every session rather than reusing a fitted model.
The partial similarity of the dynamics across sessions nonetheless made this calibration simple in practice: gains from earlier sessions served as a starting point and needed minor adjustments (Materials and Methods).

The fitted poles yield two timescales (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}F). The two modes overlap in time, so neither maps onto one feature of the response alone (in Mice 5b and 1 the slower mode contributes $60$--$68\%$ of the trough).
The dip timescale, the decay of the fastest mode (a damped 2.4--4.2\,Hz oscillation that shapes the initial suppression), was consistent across sessions (median $0.113$\,s; $0.106$--$0.114$\,s in Mice 5a, 5b and 1, and $0.14$\,s in Mouse 3; Materials and Methods), although it depends on model order: the one other model fitting almost as well ($R^2$ within $0.005$) gave $0.117$ instead of $0.106$\,s in Mouse 5a and $0.154$ instead of $0.114$\,s in Mouse 1.
The rebound timescale, the slowest mode, which governs how activity returns after the dip, varied across sessions ($0.27$\,s in Mouse 1, $0.50$\,s in Mouse 3 at the edge of the $0.5$\,s fit window, and beyond it in Mice 5a and 5b, where in Mouse 5a it adds a small sustained suppression, $6\%$ of the trough) and is correspondingly less well constrained: it exceeded the fit window in $39\%$ and $72\%$ of bootstrap refits in Mice 5a and 5b and in all refits in Mouse 3, against $3\%$ in Mouse 1 (Materials and Methods).
Accordingly, the peak-normalized responses share the dip time course and diverge only during the rebound, which was small relative to the dip (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}D).
The dip, consistent across sessions, therefore sets how quickly stimulation can move cortical activity, whereas the session-variable rebound acts on slower timescales, at which the integral term of the controller can compensate.

\subsection*{Single-trial variability reflects state-dependent ongoing activity}
The LTI model captured the trial-averaged response, but individual trials deviated from it, and the size of these deviations depended on brain state.
We characterized the state before each stimulus with three measures, all taken over the 1\,s before onset (Materials and Methods): motion energy from face video, a measure of the animal's movement; relative 2--4\,Hz ($\delta$) power, the fraction of 0.4--10\,Hz cortical power in the $\delta$ band, which reflects how strongly the spectrum is concentrated in slow rhythms; and absolute 2--4\,Hz power, which reflects the overall amplitude of slow fluctuations.
We quantified each trial's variability as the root-mean-square deviation of its response over the LTI fit window ($0$ to $0.5$\,s) from the trial-averaged response at its amplitude, which is the response the LTI model describes.
We normalized this variability within each session and binned trials by quartile of each state measure (1516 trials, four sessions; Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}G).
We computed the same measure over a stimulus-free window sampled between stimulus trials, to ask whether ongoing activity without stimulation shows the same state dependence (Materials and Methods).

In every session, single trials were least variable in the top motion quartile ($0.57$--$0.71$ times the session's typical trial), whereas the three lower quartiles stayed between $0.88$ and $1.30$, so the pattern was a drop at the highest motion rather than a graded trend (top versus bottom quartile, geometric mean over sessions $0.58$, range $0.50$--$0.81$; Figure~\ref{fig:state_quartile}A).
Variability rose from quartile to quartile with absolute $\delta$ power in all four sessions ($2.35$, range $1.70$--$2.71$), and showed no consistent pattern with relative $\delta$ power ($1.03$, range $0.83$--$1.16$).

Ongoing activity in the stimulus-free window followed the same pattern in each state (Figure~\ref{fig:figure2}G, dashed line; top versus bottom quartile $0.60$, $1.08$ and $2.70$ for motion, relative and absolute $\delta$ power), and it deviated from its own trial average about as much as the evoked response did (ratio of within-session variabilities, $0.92$--$1.06$ across sessions).
Expressed relative to the stimulus-free window, response variability changed little across quartiles (top versus bottom quartile $0.98$, $0.95$ and $0.87$; Figure~\ref{fig:state_quartile}A--C, second column); if anything, the response varied slightly less with absolute $\delta$ power than ongoing activity did.
Single-trial responses and the ongoing activity they ride on therefore changed together with state: during movement both were less variable, and with higher absolute $\delta$ power both were more variable (per-session values and supporting tests, Table~\ref{tab:stat_fig2g}).
We report this co-variation and do not separate a state-dependent change in the stimulus effect from a state-dependent change in ongoing activity.
This is consistent with brain state acting as a disturbance added to an otherwise fixed plant, which is the situation feedback control is designed to handle, and it sets a floor on how well any fixed model can predict an individual trial; we test this directly in closed loop below (Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}).

The two $\delta$ measures are correlated but not redundant (Spearman $\rho = 0.47$ over the impulse trials).
We therefore carry both into the closed-loop analysis, relative power as a candidate state variable normalized for overall power and absolute power as a measure of disturbance amplitude; there, absolute power is taken over 1--4\,Hz and all state measures are computed from $-2$ to $+3$\,s around onset (Materials and Methods). Descriptive panel; per-session values for every quartile, the trial-level distributions and the response relative to the stimulus-free control are in Figure~\ref{fig:state_quartile}, and supporting tests in Table~\ref{tab:stat_fig2g}

\subsection*{Closed-loop control improves reference-tracking accuracy}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
We used the PI controller (Figure~\ref{fig:figure1}E; Materials and Methods) to target spontaneous activity to $-5\%\,\Delta F/F$ for 3\,s per trial.
For each mouse, we first tuned the feedforward gain $K_r$ and the feedback gains $K_p$ and $K_i$ (Materials and Methods), and at the start of each session we adjusted them slightly to account for session specific changes.
We then ran a validation block with fixed gains, in which closed-loop trials and open-loop trials (feedforward only, no feedback) were randomly interleaved (Table~\ref{tab:gains}).
Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}A--D and F show one representative session (Mouse10; $K_r = 0.1$, $K_p = 0.07$, $K_i = 0.1$), and Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}E and G--I pool all 15 sessions.
On single trials, the closed-loop controller adjusted the stimulation as activity changed, whereas the open-loop input was fixed regardless of the cortical output (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}A).
As a result, trial-averaged closed-loop activity stayed close to the reference throughout stimulation, whereas open-loop activity carried the error of the feedforward calibration (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}B). The trial-averaged closed-loop input departed from the fixed open-loop input (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}C), a correction that reflects errors in the nominal model or stimulation-related biases.

We quantified tracking accuracy on each trial as the root-mean-square error (RMSE) between activity and the reference.
In the representative session, closed loop lowered both the typical RMSE and its spread across trials (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}F).
Across sessions, closed loop kept the trial-averaged error below open loop over the whole stimulation window (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}G).
To compare sessions on one scale, we divided each session's mean closed-loop RMSE by its mean open-loop RMSE in each window, so that a ratio below one means closed loop tracked better (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}H).
We tested the log ratio across sessions with a mixed model that accounts for mouse (Materials and Methods).
Over the full 3\,s window, closed loop lowered RMSE in all 15 sessions from 4 mice, by 23\% on average (geometric-mean ratio $0.77$; Table~\ref{tab:stat_fig3}).

The initial deviation, the difference between output and reference at stimulation onset, settles within 1\,s.
We therefore split the stimulation window into a transient (0--1\,s), which captures this settling, and a steady state (1--3\,s), which reflects the response to ongoing disturbances.
Closed loop lowered RMSE by 15\% in the transient (ratio $0.85$; $p = 2.0\times10^{-5}$) and by 32\% in the steady state ($0.68$; $p = 1.1\times10^{-6}$), with the ratio below one in all 15 sessions in both windows (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}H).
Before and after stimulation the ratio stayed at one (geometric mean $1.01$ and $1.00$; $p = 0.84$ and $1.0$).

\subsection*{Closed-loop control reduces trial-to-trial variability}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
In the representative session, across-trial variance was similar in the two conditions before stimulation but diverged upon stimulation onset (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}D).
Open-loop variance fell only slightly, whereas closed-loop variance fell much further, because feedback acts on each trial's deviation in real time.
Averaged across all 15 sessions, variance fell in both conditions during stimulation, but further under closed loop (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}E).
In the per-session ratio of closed- to open-loop across-trial variance (Figure~\ref{fig:figure3}I; same test as above), closed loop lowered variance by 35\% in the transient (ratio $0.65$; $p = 0.0039$) and by 46\% in the steady state ($0.54$; $p = 5.4\times10^{-5}$).
Before and after stimulation, the ratio stayed near one (geometric mean $1.04$ and $1.05$; $p = 0.98$ and $0.48$), so the reduction was confined to the period in which feedback was active.
This is consistent with disturbance rejection: feedback cancels part of the ongoing activity that differs from trial to trial, which we interpret as input from other brain areas.
The size of the improvement varied across sessions, likely reflecting differences in arousal, opsin expression and the overlap between the stimulated and recorded regions.

\subsection*{Controller performance is brain-state dependent}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
How well feedback tracked the reference depended on brain state.
We described each trial's state with four variables: the initial deviation of activity from the reference at onset, motion energy, 2--4\,Hz power relative to 0.4--10\,Hz power, and absolute 1--4\,Hz power.
Motion and power were measured from 2\,s before onset to the end of stimulation (Materials and Methods).
Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}A shows closed-loop trials, each high in one state.
Face video was available for 11 of the 15 sessions; the other 4 were excluded from all motion analyses.

Within closed-loop trials, different states mattered in the two windows (Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}B,C).
We regressed each trial's tracking error (RMSE) on the state variables within each of the 11 sessions with face video and measured how much of its trial-to-trial variation each variable explained beyond the others; the two power measures are correlated, so each entered its own model with the initial deviation and motion (Materials and Methods).
The initial deviation explained much of the variation in transient error but almost none in the steady state.
Absolute 1--4\,Hz power explained a modest part in both windows, more than any other variable in the steady state, and remained the largest when relative power was added to the same model (Figure~\ref{fig:f4_exemplars}B,C).
Relative power explained little, and motion explained nothing beyond the other variables.
Closed-loop error thus varied with large, slow fluctuations but not with motion.

We then compared open- and closed-loop tracking across the range of each state, with a mixed model fitted to steady-state RMSE over all trials (Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}D; 15 sessions from 4 mice, or 11 for motion; Materials and Methods).
We summarized each state with three slopes, each per within-session standard deviation (SD) of the state.
The open-loop slope measures \emph{predictability}: a positive slope means that error rises with the state in a way a fixed feedforward input cannot anticipate, which we treat as a disturbance.
The closed-loop slope measures \emph{regularizability}: a flat slope means that feedback tracks the reference regardless of the state.
Closed-loop error can rise with a state and still be a fixed fraction of a rising open-loop error.
We therefore also measured \emph{attenuation}, the change with state in the error ratio, the fraction of open-loop error left under feedback; a falling ratio means that feedback removes proportionally more error as the state grows.
Table~\ref{tab:fig4_state_stats} gives every estimate with its uncorrected $p$ value; the two attenuation results and the absolute-power slopes below survive correction for multiple comparisons.

In every state model, closed loop lowered error at the state's mean, and two states stood out.
Feedback attenuated the error that accompanied motion: the more the animal moved, the more of the error feedback removed (the error ratio fell by 9\% per SD, $p = 0.0048$).
Open-loop error tended to rise with motion while closed-loop error stayed flat, and the open-loop slope exceeded the closed-loop slope in all 11 sessions.
Absolute 1--4\,Hz power raised open- and closed-loop error by similar amounts, so the fraction of error left under feedback grew with power (the error ratio rose by 8\% per SD, $p = 0.0014$).
The initial deviation tended to raise both errors, though neither slope survived correction, and it left their ratio unchanged, so feedback removed the same fraction of error whether a trial began far from or near the reference.
Relative 2--4\,Hz power had small effects that did not survive correction, and we do not interpret them.
The power measures span the stimulation period, so they describe activity during stimulation, which the loop itself shapes, rather than the state in which the trial began.

Absolute 1--4\,Hz power, which indexes large, slow fluctuations, therefore accounted for more of the variation in steady-state closed-loop error than any other state (Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}C), and feedback removed proportionally less error as it grew.

\subsection*{Feedback partly rejects a disturbance predicted from the contralateral hemisphere}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
To test disturbance rejection directly, we estimated each trial's disturbance from the opposite hemisphere.
A linear predictor from contralateral pixels to the ipsilateral control readout was fit only on spontaneous, no-laser frames, so it learned how the two hemispheres covary but nothing about the stimulation (Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}E).
It generalized to held-out frames ($R^2 = 0.75$ in the session of Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}F; median $0.76$, range $0.44$--$0.98$, 13 sessions).
On a stimulation trial, the prediction gave the \emph{Global} component $G$, an estimate of what the site would have done without controller input, and the remainder of the measured response $A$ gave the \emph{Local} component $L = A - G$, what the laser and controller added on top of $G$ (Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}F).
The split is a linear projection, not a causal decomposition.
The predictor was blind to the laser but its input was not: contralateral activity also changed during stimulation, so $G$ carried part of the laser response (median $23\%$ of the readout's open-loop dip over 0--3\,s).
After subtracting the session's average leak and referencing $G$ to its pre-onset baseline, we took the remaining trial-to-trial excursion as the disturbance $D$ (Materials and Methods).

We scored rejection with a rejection ratio (RR), the residual energy of the response about the reference divided by the energy of $D$, both over the steady-state window; $\mathrm{RR} < 1$ indicates rejection.
Of the 13 sessions with a predictor, we excluded two in which a standing offset supplied more than one third of the closed-loop residual energy ($38\%$ and $59\%$; Materials and Methods).
In the remaining 11, closed loop partly rejected the disturbance (session-level mixed model, geometric-mean RR $0.58$, one-sided $p = 1.3\times10^{-4}$; 595 trials, 3 mice; session median below one in all 11; Figure~\ref{fig:figure4}G; Table~\ref{tab:stat_fig4g}).
Open loop, by contrast, left more residual energy than the disturbance it faced (median session RR $1.25$, against $0.72$ in closed loop; lower in closed loop in all 11 sessions, $p = 4.9\times10^{-4}$).
Most of this difference lay in the response level, which open loop sets only by calibration.
The standing offset supplied a median $35\%$ of the residual energy in open loop and $7\%$ in closed loop ($p = 9.8\times10^{-4}$), whereas the fluctuation energy fell less, from $0.66$ to $0.54$ of the disturbance energy ($p = 0.0024$; both lower in closed loop in 10 of 11 sessions).

\subsection*{Preview based controller tracks a sinusoidal reference}

Each session ran four randomly interleaved modes: open loop (OL), open loop with preview (OL$+$p), closed loop (CL) and closed loop with preview (CL$+$p).

The open loop the response followed the reference in shape but lagged it and varied from trial to trial (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}B; laser power in D).
The trial average kept the sinusoid in every mode, and the across-trial spread was widest under open loop (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}C).

Feedback reduced both the trial-to-trial spread and the error.
From OL to CL, across-trial variance fell from $17.9$ to $14.8$ (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}E), the time-resolved error fell with it (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}F), and median per-trial RMSE fell from $3.82$ to $2.80$\,\%\,$\Delta F/F$ (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}G).

Preview corrected the lag, the delay of the response behind the reference.
The response lagged the reference by $60^\circ$ under OL and $36^\circ$ under CL, and preview moved both close to zero, with or without feedback ($5^\circ$ and $-9^\circ$; Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}H).
The open-loop shift matched the five-sample lookahead, as the design predicts; pooled over the three sessions, the median per-trial shift was $5.0$ samples.
Preview did not lower the error as feedback did: median RMSE changed little from OL to OL$+$p ($3.82$ and $3.55$), and adding preview to closed loop did not improve it (CL $2.80$, CL$+$p $3.30$; Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}G).

With three sessions we report no significance tests and take the direction of each effect and its consistency across sessions as the result.
In each session, per-trial RMSE (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}I) and across-trial variance (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}K) were lower under closed loop than open loop, and preview canceled the lag (Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}J).
Pooled over sessions, mean per-trial RMSE fell from $4.58$ under OL to $3.83$ under CL, with no comparable change from preview (OL$+$p $4.47$, CL$+$p $3.95$).
CL$+$p, the controller with both feedback and preview, therefore removed the lag while keeping its error below open loop, and it had the narrowest spread of per-trial RMSE of the four modes, in the detailed session and pooled over sessions (SD $1.57$ and $1.58$).

\subsection*{A model-predictive controller optimizes reference tracking on data replay}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
The PI controller corrects instantaneous error but is blind to incoming disturbances.
% A model-predictive controller (MPC) can anticipate the part of ongoing activity that a model predicts.
A model-predictive controller (MPC;~\citealp{rawlings2017model}) can anticipate both the reference and the part of ongoing activity that a model predicts.
At every frame our MPC predicted the controlled site over the next second and chose the laser plan that best tracked the reference without abrupt command changes (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}A,B).
Its predictions came from a linear vector autoregressive model with exogenous input (VARX;~\citealp{lutkepohl2005new}) built from the past second of the controlled site, 100 sites over the cortex and the stimulation.
On held-out open-loop trials the VARX predicted the controlled site better than the last observed value for a short horizon forward in time, and the 100 cortical sites improved on a model of the site and laser alone (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}H; Figure~\ref{fig:supp_varx}).

Running MPC in real time needs new software and hardware on the rig, so we replayed recorded closed-loop trials with the MPC in place of the rig's controller.
Each replay drove a VARX fitted to the whole session with the trial's recorded one-step prediction residuals (Materials and Methods).
We use this model to predict activity over a short horizon( Figure~\ref{fig:supp_varx}).
The MPC's own model never saw a closed-loop trial.
All results in this section are descriptive simulations.

In one session with a constant reference (Mouse~1), the MPC lowered the median per-trial RMSE over the steady-state window (1--3\,s) from $1.46$ under the recorded PI controller to $0.88\%\,\Delta F/F$, and its RMSE was lower on 107 of 108 trials (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}C--E).
Across-trial variance over the same window fell by $37\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}F), and the RMSE gain held across the command-smoothness penalties we tried (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}G).
With perfect prediction (the replay model and the trial's future residuals), the MPC reached $0.27\%\,\Delta F/F$, so most of the error it left was activity its model could not predict or attributed to constrained inputs

With the sinusoidal reference the MPC also lowered the error, but by less.
We replayed the 57 CL$+$p trials of the representative Mouse~3 session of Figure~\ref{fig:figure5}, the rig mode that, like the MPC, combined feedback with advance knowledge of the reference.
Median per-trial RMSE over the 4\,s window fell from $3.00$ under CL$+$p to $2.67\%\,\Delta F/F$, and the MPC's RMSE was lower on 56 of 57 trials (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}I--K).
Across-trial variance fell to $0.71$ times that of CL$+$p (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}L), and as with preview the phase lag stayed near zero, against $71^\circ$ under open loop (Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_direct}M).
