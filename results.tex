\section*{Results}
% %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

We developed a real-time, closed-loop optogenetic system to regulate population activity within a targeted cortical region monitored by wide-field calcium imaging. Trial-averaged cortical responses to optogenetic stimulation were well described by a stationary linear input--output map, providing the empirical foundation for controller design. We show  an effective controller design and subsequently show the state dependence of controller performance.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Experimental setup and closed-loop control architecture}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
We performed closed-loop experiments in head-fixed transgenic mice co-expressing GCaMP and an inhibitory opsin, positioned in a behavioral chamber \citep{Matveev2024}. The forelimbs of each mouse rested on a freely moving wheel, and a blank screen faced the animal, no visual stimuli was presented. We recorded neural activity at 35\,Hz using wide-field calcium imaging (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}A) and delivered spatially resolved optogenetic stimulation to a cortical region adjacent to the $\Delta F/F$ output kernel (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}B). We chose the stimulation region to overlap closely with the output kernel (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}C) to directly suppress activity within that region~\citep{Matveev2024}. In a subset of experiments, we captured motion energy(see Methods) from face video, defined as pixel variation squared per unit time.

We designed an end-to-end architecture interacting with hardware and software components for signal processing and fast closed-loop control of the mesoscale activity (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}D). The software layer implemented the framework required to compute the control signal from real-time feedback. Specifically, we implemented a proportional--integral (PI) feedback controller with open-loop (feedforward) compensation to drive cortical activity toward a reference $\Delta F/F$ value (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}E). The activity we refer to is the DF/F of a kernel on a region of interest of the cortex. In this paper the output state we refer is this DF/F at any time.
The feedforward component supplied the nominal open-loop stimulus, learnt using stimulation experiments, required to reach the reference state, computed from the calibrated controller gains, while the PI feedback component provided real-time error correction, compensating for bias arising from inaccurate feedforward gains, residual nonlinearities, and spontaneous trial-to-trial fluctuations.

In this architecture (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}E), the kernel-averaged wide-field measurement serves as the system output; the controller computes an optogenetic stimulus from the error between the measured output and the reference, and this input drives the dynamics, subject to a maximum system delay of $47$~ms (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}F). This delay bounds the bandwidth over which feedback can act (see Discussion).

\subsection*{Trial-averaged cortical dynamics are well approximated by a linear system}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Trial-averaged cortical responses to optogenetic stimulation followed a linear input--output map, and a low-dimensional linear time-invariant (LTI) system closely approximated the impulse response. We delivered optogenetic impulse inputs at discrete amplitudes spanning 0--1.8\,mW, randomly interleaved across trials within each session. We repeated this experiment for 4 sessions across 3 mice.

The mean total inhibition energy per trial (see Materials and Methods) grew monotonically. We observed that stronger the stimulation power, stronger the suppression in activity at trial-averaged scale. At trial-to-trial level no such relationship can be established due to variability. We show that the input to output relationship can be approximated by a linear relationship (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}A; slopes $-0.34, -0.57, -0.59, -0.96$ and $R^2 = 0.97, 0.51, 0.92, 0.91$). This monotonic relationship between stimulation amplitude and inhibition energy supports the design of a feedforward controller calibrated from impulse-response data \citep{astrom1995pid}. Without this linearity a guarantee on controller stability can not be established.
We show that the trial-averaged dynamical response to stimulus can be approximated by a LTI system.
Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}B shows the trial-averaged impulse responses for one session, from which we estimated a settling time of $\sim$200\,ms. Fitting the model to a held-out subset of trials and predicting the remaining trials recovered the measured response at every amplitude (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}C), and this generalization held within each session. The trial-cross-validated held-out $R^2$ had a pooled median of $0.86$ across the four sessions (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}D). The variability of responses within a session was well evident based on range or $R^2$ seen across heldout trials. The fitted dynamics were session-specific as applying each session's model to every other session, with only the scalar gain re-fit per target, yielded high self-prediction along the diagonal but weaker off-diagonal transfer (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}E). This indicates that the impulse response must be identified per session rather than assumed fixed. Each response was summarized by two time constants (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}F), a fast and a slow pole of the LTI system. The fast timescale constant of ${\sim}0.14$\,s was tightly reproducible across sessions ($0.14$--$0.15$\,s in three of four sessions) and consistent with the ${\sim}200$\,ms settling time, and a slower constant (${\sim}0.2$--$0.3$\,s) that was more variable. Refitting a low-order model on a common amplitude range shared by all sessions confirmed that this dominant timescale ($\tau \approx 0.14$\,s) was statistically indistinguishable between sessions—the between session spread was smaller than the within-session bootstrap uncertainty—so the preparation shares a characteristic inhibitory timescale even though the full fitted models are session-specific (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}F).

\subsection*{Stimulation response is State dependent}
% Although the LTI model captured the trial-averaged response, individual trials deviated from it in a brain-state-dependent manner. Normalizing each trial's prediction error(LTI model's prediction) within its session and binning trials by the pre-stimulus state (1767 trials, four sessions), we found that per-trial prediction error was smaller on high-motion energy trials (top- versus bottom-quartile ratio $0.71$, 95\% CI $[0.60, 0.84]$; session-aware mixed model $p = 1.5\times10^{-7}$, 4/4 sessions) and larger on trials with elevated relative 2--4\,Hz power (ratio $1.10$, $[0.94, 1.27]$; $p = 0.005$, 3/4 sessions; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}G). We used relative rather than absolute 2--4\,Hz power because absolute power co-varies with overall signal amplitude---its larger quartile ratio ($2.04$) is shown but not interpreted as an independent state effect. This residual, state-dependent component sets a floor on how well any single fixed model can predict an individual trial---a limit that motivates the closed-loop, feedback-based approach developed next.
Although the LTI model captured the trial-averaged response, individual trials deviated from it in a brain-state-dependent manner. We normalized each trial's deviation from the LTI model's prediction within its session and binned trials by pre-stimulus state (1767 trials, four sessions; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}G). Each state was also tested against a stimulus-free control constructed identically from the pre-onset window, which separates an effect on how the stimulus is processed from an effect on the amplitude of ongoing activity.

The three states dissociated. Prediction error was smaller on high-motion trials (top- versus bottom-quartile ratio $0.71$, 95\% CI $[0.60, 0.84]$; session-aware mixed model $p = 1.5\times10^{-7}$, 4/4 sessions), and fell more steeply than the stimulus-free control over the same quartiles ($0.71$ versus $0.79$), so movement alters the evoked response itself. Elevated relative 2--4\,Hz power raised prediction error modestly ($1.10$, $[0.94, 1.27]$; $p = 0.005$, 3/4 sessions) while leaving the control flat (ratio $1.00$, $\rho = +0.001$, $p = 0.96$)---a small effect, but one that is independent of signal power. Absolute 2--4\,Hz power produced much the steepest trend ($1.99$, $[1.69, 2.37]$; $p < 10^{-16}$, 4/4 sessions), and its stimulus-free control rose equally steeply (ratio $2.10$, $\rho = +0.37$), identifying it as a change in the amplitude of ongoing activity rather than in the stimulus response. The two $\delta$ measures are correlated but far from redundant (Spearman $\rho = 0.55$) and order trials differently: absolute power is high on trials carrying large slow swings, relative power on trials whose spectrum is concentrated in the 2--4\,Hz band. We therefore carry both forward, treating relative power as the power-independent state variable and absolute power as the disturbance-amplitude variable. This residual, state-dependent component sets a floor on how well any single fixed model can predict an individual trial.

\subsection*{Closed-loop control improves reference-tracking accuracy}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
We deploy our closed loop controller(Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}), where we set target of $-5\%$DF/F for the output activity. We apply the PI controller for a trial length of 3seconds and interleave the trials such that an open-loop or closed loop control operation is performed randomly on a trial. Across 15 sessions from 4 mice, with confirmed opsin expression, closed-loop feedback consistently improved the tracking of a reference target activity relative to open-loop stimulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}). Under open-loop, the feedforward term produced responses that regularly deviated from the reference, reflecting the inability of a fixed input to compensate for ongoing spontaneous fluctuations or account for inaccuracies in the estimated open-loop controller. Under closed-loop control, the proportional term corrected instantaneous deviations, while the integral term corrected for persistent offset, yielding substantially improved tracking of the target reference at the single-trial level (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}A).

Trial-averaged responses show that closed-loop activity tracked the reference closely throughout the stimulation window, whereas open-loop responses reflected the inaccuracies in tuning (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}B). The difference in mean input between conditions (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}C) demonstrates the additional corrective signal contributed by real-time feedback.
We quantified controller accuracy per trial using Root Mean Squared Error(RMSE) of output against the target reference. 
The per-trial RMSE spread comparison of both controllers indicates both improved mean performance and greater reliability of neural modulation across trials (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}E). These improvements held across all recorded sessions: averaging the tracking error over the 15 sessions showed the same gap open at stimulation onset and persist for the rest of the trial (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}G), and the open-loop/closed-loop RMSE ratio exceeded one across the stimulation window not before or after (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}I). We split the stimulation window analysis into 0--1s and 1--3s window as initial deviation settles before 1s so 1--3s reflects a purely disturbance affected dynamic. 
For the session shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}A--E we used $K_{r} = 0.1$, $K_{p} = 0.07$, and $K_{i} = 0.1$. The sufficiency of such small gains suggests that the approximately linear, stationary input--output structure identified empirically was adequate to support stable PI feedback.

\subsection*{Closed-loop control reduces trial-to-trial variability}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Feedback also made the responses more repeatable. We measured the variance across trials at each time point within the trial (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}D). Before the laser turned on the two conditions were indistinguishable, as they have to be---nothing separates them until stimulation starts. During stimulation, open-loop variance dropped only slightly below its own baseline, because a fixed input cannot respond to whatever the cortex happens to be doing on a given trial. Closed-loop variance dropped considerably further. The proportional term acts on each trial's own deviation as it appears, so trials that would otherwise have scattered are pulled back toward the reference and the spread across trials narrows.

The same pattern held across the cohort. Pooling the within-session variance traces over all 15 sessions reproduced the single-session result, with the gap between conditions opening at stimulation onset and closing after it (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}F). To check that this was specific to the stimulation window and not a by-product of stimulating at all, we took the open-loop/closed-loop variance ratio in four windows: before the trial, the first second of stimulation, the remaining two seconds, and after stimulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}H). The ratio exceeded one in both stimulation windows and in neither of the surrounding windows, placing the variance reduction where the feedback is active.

We tested every open-loop--closed-loop comparison with a session-aware linear mixed model---condition as a fixed effect, random per-session condition slopes, and a mouse random effect---because the 15 sessions come from only four mice, and treating them as independent would overstate the evidence. Closed-loop control lowered both tracking RMSE and trial-to-trial variance in the early and late stimulation windows (all $p < 10^{-6}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}H,I). Per-session Wilcoxon signed-rank tests agreed in every case, and the between-mouse variance was negligible, so no single animal is carrying the effect.

The validity of these results depends on the trial-averaging assumption holding across the session; the empirical variance convergence in Fig.~\ref{fig:S1} is consistent with this assumption over the validation period. Performance varied across animals and sessions, likely reflecting residual non-stationarity in arousal and behavioral state, differences in opsin expression, and session-to-session variation in the spatial overlap between the stimulation and output regions. Despite this variability, the direction of improvement---in both tracking accuracy and trial-to-trial variance---was consistent across all experiments.

\subsection*{Disturbance attribution: motion and low-frequency state}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
% Motion figure removed 2026-09-29 (user): its content is covered by Fig 4D. Motion numbers
% recomputed on the correct 7 motion-capture sessions (was mislabeled 11); see RESEARCH 2026-09-29.
To identify what drives residual trial-to-trial tracking error, we first asked whether movement explained high-error trials and whether closed-loop feedback rejected it. Simultaneous motion capture was available for 7 of the 11 closed-loop sessions (397 closed-loop trials); the remaining four sessions had no usable motion trace and are excluded from all motion analyses. Regressing per-trial tracking RMSE on concurrent motion energy within each session, with both quantities z-scored within session so slopes are comparable, open-loop error rose with motion (median slope $+0.16$ standard-deviation units of RMSE per standard-deviation of motion; Wilcoxon signed-rank $p = 0.016$), whereas under closed-loop control this dependence was abolished (median slope $-0.04$; $p = 0.30$). The open- versus closed-loop slope difference was significant (signed-rank $p = 0.016$; the open-loop slope exceeded the closed-loop slope in all 7 sessions). Movement is therefore a principal disturbance under open-loop stimulation that feedback specifically rejects, decoupling tracking accuracy from motor state (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}D).

A different, low-frequency disturbance set the error feedback could not remove. Relative 2--4\,Hz power of the readout (2--4\,Hz band power divided by 0.4--10\,Hz total power, over the $-2$\,s to trial-end window; a power-independent measure) uniquely explained the settled closed-loop error (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}C), and closed-loop error climbed across its quartiles while open-loop error stayed flat (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}D). Unlike movement, this ongoing low-frequency cortical fluctuation is not rejected by feedback---it is the component of the brain state that sets the irreducible closed-loop error.

\subsection*{Feedback rejects a state-dependent disturbance carried by the contralateral hemisphere}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Because the two hemispheres are strongly co-active, spontaneous activity in the stimulated (ipsilateral) region is well predicted from the opposite hemisphere. We fit a stimulation-blind linear predictor from contralateral pixels to the ipsilateral control readout on spontaneous, no-laser epochs (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}E); it generalized to held-out pre-stimulus windows (contralateral$\rightarrow$ipsilateral $R^2 = 0.85$ open loop and $0.89$ closed loop; representative session), giving a trial-by-trial readout of the ongoing brain state the controller must contend with.

% Fig-4 lettering A-G (2026-09-29): A state exemplars | B error-decomposition model | C unique R^2 |
% D four states vs performance (stitched quartiles, predictability/controllability arrows) |
% E contra kernel map | F A=G+L model example | G CL disturbance rejection (reach-gated RR, all sessions).

To identify which state factors drive the residual closed-loop error, we decomposed per-trial tracking error (RMSE) into the unique contribution of four pre-stimulus factors---initial deviation, motion energy, relative 2--4\,Hz power, and absolute 2--4\,Hz power (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}A,B; exemplar trials for every session in Fig.~\ref{fig:S3})---resolved by error window (early transient, 0--1\,s; settled, 1--3\,s; 397 closed-loop trials, 7 sessions with motion capture). The composition turned over between windows. The initial deviation dominated the transient but its unique contribution collapsed once activity settled (unique $R^2 = 0.29 \rightarrow 0.004$), whereas relative and absolute 2--4\,Hz power carried the settled window (unique $R^2 = 0.10 \rightarrow 0.12$ and $0.23 \rightarrow 0.35$). Motion contributed negligibly in either window (unique $R^2 < 0.01$), consistent with feedback having already rejected the motor disturbance (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}D). The error that feedback cannot remove is therefore not a transient leftover but a distinct quantity tied to ongoing low-frequency activity (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}C); this composition is robust to how the low-frequency factor is defined (Fig.~\ref{fig:S4}).

We then resolved the mechanism at the single-trial level. Applying the predictor on every stimulation trial defines the \emph{Global} component $G$---the activity the ipsilateral region would have shown with no controller input, that is, the disturbance---and the \emph{Local} component $L = A - G$, the effect of the laser and controller on top of it (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}F). Across trials the actual response was well reconstructed as the Global disturbance plus the average local response (cross-validated $R^2 = 0.85$ open loop, $0.78$ closed loop), confirming that $A = G + L$ captures the single-trial response. We then quantified how well closed-loop control rejects this disturbance with a per-trial energy ratio $\mathrm{RR} = \|A - r\|^2 / \|D\|^2$: the residual energy of the actual response about the reference ($r = -5\%\,\Delta F/F$) divided by the energy of the disturbance $D$---the contra-predicted excursion the controller must cancel, referenced to its own no-laser baseline (leak-corrected)---over the settled window (1--3\,s, after the inhibitory onset transient). Referencing the disturbance to its own baseline rather than to the target keeps the fixed setpoint offset from being counted as disturbance, so that $\mathrm{RR} < 1$ means the controlled response carries less energy than the disturbance it counters. Closed-loop control rejected the disturbance across the population: the geometric-mean ratio was $\mathrm{RR} = 0.58$ (95\% CI $[0.48, 0.71]$), below one by a session-aware mixed model ($\log\mathrm{RR} \sim 1 + (1|\mathrm{mouse}) + (1|\mathrm{mouse{:}session})$; one-sided $p = 2.1\times10^{-8}$, $n = 11$ sessions; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}G). Two sessions in which the controller never reached the setpoint---so the residual is dominated by a standing setpoint error rather than by disturbance rejection---were excluded.

Finally, we made these regimes explicit by binning trials into quartiles of each pre-stimulus state (low to high) and reading off two properties from a session-aware mixed model (RMSE regressed on condition, state and their interaction, with random per-session intercepts and open/closed-loop slopes and a mouse intercept; $n = 11$ sessions, $7$ for motion; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}D). We summarise each state by its effect on \emph{predictability}---the open-loop disturbance, read from the trend of the uncontrolled error---and on \emph{controllability}, or steerability---read from the trend of the rejected fraction, the open-minus-closed-loop gap (the condition-by-state interaction). Closed-loop control reduced tracking error strongly at every state (open-minus-closed-loop gap at mean state $p < 10^{-6}$ for all four factors). The states differed in how that benefit moved. Motion made the disturbance less predictable---open-loop error rose with motion ($p = 9\times10^{-5}$)---yet more controllable: the loop rejected a growing share of it and the gap widened ($p = 0.004$), the signature of feedback rejecting an unanticipated disturbance. Relative 2--4\,Hz power did the opposite: the open loop was unaffected while closed-loop error climbed, so controllability fell ($p = 0.018$), identifying the ongoing low-frequency fluctuation as the one disturbance feedback cannot fully cancel. The initial deviation and absolute 2--4\,Hz power lowered predictability (open-loop error rose, $p < 10^{-6}$) but left controllability unchanged---disturbances that scale both conditions together. The controller therefore behaves as an internal model whose rejection is steerable for the unanticipated motor disturbance but limited for the ongoing low-frequency brain state that sets the residual error.

\subsection*{Feedforward control for variable reference tracking}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Feedback and preview correct different failures of sinusoidal tracking: feedback removes trial-to-trial variability, while preview removes the phase lag imposed by the plant. We asked the controller to track a 1\,Hz sinusoidal reference for 4\,s in a dual-opsin mouse (AL\_0048, right hemisphere, inhibitory opsin), and crossed two factors in a $2\times2$ design---feedback on or off, and preview on or off---giving four modes: open-loop (OL), open-loop with preview (OL$+$p), closed-loop (CL), and closed-loop with preview (CL$+$p). The preview block shifts the reference forward by a fixed lookahead before it reaches the controller, compensating the delay of the cortical response rather than its amplitude (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}A). We report one representative session in detail (200 sinusoidal trials; $n = 51, 46, 46, 57$ trials for OL, OL$+$p, CL, CL$+$p) and the consistency of each effect across three sessions.

Open-loop tracking followed the reference in shape but lagged it and drifted between trials (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}B,C). Adding feedback reduced per-trial tracking error: median RMSE fell from $3.82$ to $2.80$\,\%\,$\Delta F/F$ between OL and CL (rank-sum $p = 7.7\times10^{-4}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}G), and across-trial variance fell from $17.9$ to $14.8$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}E). The same ordering held in every session tested (CL $<$ OL for both RMSE and variance in 3/3 sessions; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}I,K), and pooling per-trial RMSE across sessions confirmed it (OL $4.58 \pm 1.77$, $n = 136$; CL $3.83 \pm 1.94$, $n = 100$; rank-sum $p = 1.5\times10^{-4}$; Kruskal--Wallis across all four modes $\chi^2 = 19.6$, $p = 2.1\times10^{-4}$). A repeated-measures test treating session as the unit is underpowered at $n = 3$ and did not reach significance (Friedman $p = 0.060$), so we treat the direction, not the session-level test, as the result.

Preview cancelled the phase lag but did not by itself improve tracking error. The cortical response lagged the reference by $60^\circ$ under open loop and by $36^\circ$ under closed loop; adding preview moved both to approximately zero ($5^\circ$ and $-9^\circ$ respectively; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}H). The correction matched the lookahead by construction: a five-sample preview at 35\,Hz corresponds to $143$\,ms, or $51^\circ$ at the 1\,Hz drive frequency, and the measured open-loop correction across all pooled trials was $5.0$ samples ($51.4^\circ$, median). Lag cancellation replicated in all three sessions (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}J). Tracking error, however, was unchanged by preview (OL versus OL$+$p, median RMSE $3.82$ versus $3.55$, $p = 0.32$; pooled across sessions $p = 0.44$), and preview did not further improve closed-loop error (CL versus CL$+$p, $p = 0.093$). Closed-loop control with preview did reduce error relative to open loop ($p = 0.020$) and produced the tightest distribution of the four modes (SD $1.57$ versus $1.85$, $2.75$ and $2.19$ for OL, OL$+$p and CL), but the error reduction is attributable to feedback and the lag correction to preview.

Decomposing the error clarifies why the two mechanisms do not overlap. Because the stimulation window contains exactly four cycles of the drive, per-trial squared error partitions without loss into a constant-offset term, a term at the drive frequency, and the remaining broadband term. Averaged over the three sessions, preview reduced the error at the drive frequency roughly threefold in every comparison (OL $3.14 \rightarrow$ OL$+$p $0.89$; CL $1.78 \rightarrow$ CL$+$p $1.22$\,(\%\,$\Delta F/F)^2$), while feedback reduced the broadband term ($10.55 \rightarrow 6.62$ from OL to CL). Preview therefore acts on the component of error that a fixed phase relationship can remove, and feedback on the component generated by ongoing spontaneous activity. Closed-loop control with preview was the only mode to engage both, and had the lowest total error power in two of three sessions.
