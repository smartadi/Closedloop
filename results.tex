\section*{Results}
% %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

We developed a real-time, closed-loop optogenetic system to regulate population activity within a targeted cortical region monitored by wide-field calcium imaging. Trial-averaged cortical responses to optogenetic stimulation were well described by a stationary linear input--output map, providing the empirical foundation for controller design. We show  an effective controller design and subsequently show the state dependence of controller performance.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Experimental setup and closed-loop control architecture}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
We performed closed-loop experiments in head-fixed transgenic mice co-expressing GCaMP and an inhibitory opsin, positioned in a behavioral chamber \citep{Matveev2024}. The forelimbs of each mouse rested on a freely moving wheel, and a blank screen faced the animal, no visual stimuli was presented. We recorded neural activity at 35\,Hz using wide-field calcium imaging (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}A) and delivered spatially resolved optogenetic stimulation to a cortical region adjacent to the $\Delta F/F$ output kernel (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}B). We chose the stimulation region to overlap closely with the output kernel (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}C) to directly suppress activity within that region~\citep{Matveev2024}. In a subset of experiments, we captured motion energy(see Methods) from face video, defined as pixel variation squared per unit time.

We designed an end-to-end architecture interacting with hardware and software components for signal processing and fast closed-loop control of the mesoscale activity (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}D). The software layer implemented the framework required to compute the control signal from real-time feedback. Specifically, we implemented a proportional--integral (PI) feedback controller with open-loop (feedforward) compensation to drive cortical activity toward a reference $\Delta F/F$ value (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}E). The activity we refer to is the DF/F of a kernel on a region of interest of the cortex. In this paper the output state we refer is this DF/F at any time.
The feedforward component supplied the nominal open-loop stimulus, learnt using stimulation experiments, required to reach the reference state, computed from the calibrated controller gains, while the PI feedback component provided real-time error correction, compensating for bias arising from inaccurate feedforward gains, residual nonlinearities, and spontaneous trial-to-trial fluctuations.

In this architecture (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}E), the kernel-averaged wide-field measurement serves as the system output; the controller computes an optogenetic stimulus from the error between the measured output and the reference, and this input drives the dynamics, subject to a maximum system delay of $47$~ms (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}F). This delay bounds the bandwidth over which feedback can act (see Discussion).

\subsection*{Trial-averaged cortical dynamics are well approximated by a linear system}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Trial-averaged cortical responses to optogenetic stimulation followed a linear input--output map, and a low-dimensional linear time-invariant (LTI) system closely approximated the impulse response. We delivered optogenetic impulse inputs at discrete amplitudes spanning 0--1.8\,mW, randomly interleaved across trials within each session. We repeated this experiment for 4 sessions across 3 mice.

The mean evoked suppression per trial (see Materials and Methods) grew monotonically. We observed that stronger the stimulation power, stronger the suppression in activity at trial-averaged scale. At trial-to-trial level no such relationship can be established due to variability. We show that the input to output relationship can be approximated by a linear relationship (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}A; slopes $-0.30, -0.50, -0.52, -0.81$ and $R^2 = 0.97, 0.51, 0.92, 0.92$). This monotonic relationship between stimulation amplitude and evoked suppression supports the design of a feedforward controller calibrated from impulse-response data \citep{astrom1995pid}. Without this linearity a guarantee on controller stability can not be established.
We show that the trial-averaged dynamical response to stimulus can be approximated by a LTI system.
Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}B shows the trial-averaged impulse responses for one session, from which we estimated a settling time of $\sim$200\,ms. Fitting the model to a held-out subset of trials and predicting the remaining trials recovered the measured response at every amplitude (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}C), and this generalization held within each session. The trial-cross-validated held-out $R^2$ had a pooled median of $0.86$ across the four sessions (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}D). The variability of responses within a session was well evident based on range or $R^2$ seen across heldout trials. The fitted dynamics were session-specific as applying each session's model to every other session, with only the scalar gain re-fit per target, yielded high self-prediction along the diagonal but weaker off-diagonal transfer (self $R^2 = 0.99$ versus cross-session $0.71$; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}E). This indicates that the impulse response must be identified per session rather than assumed fixed. Each response was summarized by two time constants (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}F), a fast and a slow pole of the LTI system. The fast timescale constant of ${\sim}0.14$\,s was tightly reproducible across sessions ($0.14$--$0.15$\,s in three of four sessions) and consistent with the ${\sim}200$\,ms settling time, and a slower constant that was far more variable, spanning several-fold across the four sessions ($0.18$, $0.30$, $0.33$ and $0.54$\,s). Refitting a low-order model on a common amplitude range shared by all sessions confirmed that this dominant timescale ($\tau \approx 0.14$\,s) was statistically indistinguishable between sessions—the between session spread was smaller than the within-session bootstrap uncertainty—so the preparation shares a characteristic inhibitory timescale even though the full fitted models are session-specific (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}F).

\subsection*{Stimulation response is State dependent}
Although the LTI model captured the trial-averaged response, individual trials deviated from it in a brain-state-dependent manner. We normalized each trial's deviation from the LTI model's prediction within its session and binned trials by pre-stimulus state (1767 trials, four sessions; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}G). Each state was also tested against a stimulus-free control constructed identically from the pre-onset window, which separates an effect on how the stimulus is processed from an effect on the amplitude of ongoing activity.

The three states dissociated. Prediction error was smaller on high-motion trials (top- versus bottom-quartile ratio $0.73$, 95\% CI $[0.62, 0.88]$; session-aware mixed model $p = 7.3\times10^{-7}$, 4/4 sessions), and fell more steeply than the stimulus-free control over the same quartiles ($0.73$ versus $0.80$), so movement alters the evoked response itself. Elevated relative 2--4\,Hz power raised prediction error only slightly: the quartile ratio is close to one ($1.02$, $[0.88, 1.19]$), so we report this state as a continuous effect, where it is significant (Spearman $\rho = +0.093$, $p = 8.6\times10^{-5}$; session-aware mixed model $p = 0.005$, 3/4 sessions). Its stimulus-free control stays flat (ratio $1.00$, $\rho = +0.008$, $p = 0.74$)---a small effect, but one that is independent of signal power. Absolute 2--4\,Hz power produced much the steepest trend ($2.09$, $[1.76, 2.57]$; $p < 10^{-16}$, 4/4 sessions), and its stimulus-free control rose at least as steeply (ratio $2.25$, $\rho = +0.38$), identifying it as a change in the amplitude of ongoing activity rather than in the stimulus response. The two $\delta$ measures are correlated but far from redundant (Spearman $\rho = 0.55$) and order trials differently: absolute power is high on trials carrying large slow swings, relative power on trials whose spectrum is concentrated in the 2--4\,Hz band. We therefore carry both forward, treating relative power as the power-independent state variable and absolute power as the disturbance-amplitude variable. This residual, state-dependent component sets a floor on how well any single fixed model can predict an individual trial.

\subsection*{Closed-loop control improves reference-tracking accuracy}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
We deploy our closed loop controller(Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}), where we set target of $-5\%$DF/F for the output activity. We apply the PI controller for a trial length of 3seconds and interleave the trials such that an open-loop or closed loop control operation is performed randomly on a trial. Across 15 sessions from 4 mice, with confirmed opsin expression, closed-loop feedback consistently improved the tracking of a reference target activity relative to open-loop stimulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}). Under open-loop, the feedforward term produced responses that regularly deviated from the reference, reflecting the inability of a fixed input to compensate for ongoing spontaneous fluctuations or account for inaccuracies in the estimated open-loop controller. Under closed-loop control, the proportional term corrected instantaneous deviations, while the integral term corrected for persistent offset, yielding substantially improved tracking of the target reference at the single-trial level (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}A).

Trial-averaged responses show that closed-loop activity tracked the reference closely throughout the stimulation window, whereas open-loop responses reflected the inaccuracies in tuning (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}B). The difference in mean input between conditions (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}C) demonstrates the additional corrective signal contributed by real-time feedback.
We quantified controller accuracy per trial using Root Mean Squared Error(RMSE) of output against the target reference. 
The per-trial RMSE spread comparison of both controllers indicates both improved mean performance and greater reliability of neural modulation across trials (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}E). These improvements held across all recorded sessions: averaging the tracking error over the 15 sessions showed the same gap open at stimulation onset and persist for the rest of the trial (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}G), and the open-loop/closed-loop RMSE ratio exceeded one across the stimulation window not before or after (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}I). We split the stimulation window analysis into 0--1s and 1--3s window as initial deviation settles before 1s so 1--3s reflects a purely disturbance affected dynamic. 
For the session shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}A--E we used $K_{r} = 0.1$, $K_{p} = 0.07$, and $K_{i} = 0.1$. The sufficiency of such small gains suggests that the approximately linear, stationary input--output structure identified empirically was adequate to support stable PI feedback.

\subsection*{Closed-loop control reduces trial-to-trial variability}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Feedback also made the responses more repeatable. We measured the variance across trials at each time point within the trial (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}D). Before the laser turned on the two conditions were indistinguishable, as they have to be---nothing separates them until stimulation starts. During stimulation, open-loop variance dropped only slightly below its own baseline, because a fixed input cannot respond to whatever the cortex happens to be doing on a given trial. Closed-loop variance dropped considerably further. The proportional term acts on each trial's own deviation as it appears, so trials that would otherwise have scattered are pulled back toward the reference and the spread across trials narrows.

The same pattern held across the cohort. Pooling the within-session variance traces over all 15 sessions reproduced the single-session result, with the gap between conditions opening at stimulation onset and closing after it (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}F). To check that this was specific to the stimulation window and not a by-product of stimulating at all, we took the open-loop/closed-loop variance ratio in four windows: before the trial, the first second of stimulation, the remaining two seconds, and after stimulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}H). The ratio exceeded one in both stimulation windows and in neither of the surrounding windows, placing the variance reduction where the feedback is active.

We tested every open-loop--closed-loop comparison with a session-aware linear mixed model---condition as a fixed effect, random per-session condition slopes, and a mouse random effect---because the 15 sessions come from only four mice, and treating them as independent would overstate the evidence. Closed-loop control lowered both tracking RMSE and trial-to-trial variance in the transient and steady-state windows (all $p < 5\times10^{-4}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}H,I). Per-session Wilcoxon signed-rank tests agreed in every case, and the between-mouse variance was negligible, so no single animal is carrying the effect.

The validity of these results depends on the trial-averaging assumption holding across the session; the convergence of the variance estimate in Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial_spread}C is consistent with this assumption over the validation period. Performance varied across animals and sessions, likely reflecting residual non-stationarity in arousal and behavioral state, differences in opsin expression, and session-to-session variation in the spatial overlap between the stimulation and output regions. Despite this variability, the direction of improvement---in both tracking accuracy and trial-to-trial variance---was consistent across all experiments.

\subsection*{Controller performance is brain-state dependent}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Closed-loop control lowered tracking error in every session, but the error it
left behind was not constant: single trials departed from the session mean in a
way that tracked the state of the cortex around the trial. We characterised
that state by four quantities---the initial deviation from the reference,
motion energy, relative 2--4\,Hz power, and absolute 1--4\,Hz power. The initial
deviation is read at stimulus onset; the other three are measured over one
common window, from 2\,s before onset to the end of stimulation at $+3$\,s,
because the controller's performance over a trial is shaped both by activity
before the stimulus begins and by activity while it is delivered. Representative closed-loop trials selected to be extreme in
one factor and unremarkable in the others are shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}A, with exemplars for every session in
Fig.~\ref{fig:f4_exemplars}A. Motion capture was available for 11 of the 15 sessions; all
11 enter the error decomposition below (613 closed-loop trials), and the 4 sessions with no video are excluded
from every motion analysis.

We decomposed per-trial tracking error (RMSE) into the unique contribution of
the four factors (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}B), resolved separately in the transient
(0--1\,s) and steady-state (1--3\,s) windows (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}C). The
composition turned over between the two. The initial deviation dominated the
transient and then collapsed once the response reached steady state (unique
$R^2 = 0.41 \rightarrow 0.03$), whereas relative 2--4\,Hz and absolute 1--4\,Hz
power carried the steady-state window ($0.15 \rightarrow 0.19$ and
$0.28 \rightarrow 0.44$). Motion contributed negligibly in either window (unique
$R^2 < 0.01$). The error that feedback cannot remove is therefore not a leftover
of the transient but a distinct quantity tied to ongoing low-frequency activity,
and this composition is robust to how the low-frequency factor is defined
(Fig.~\ref{fig:f4_exemplars}B--D).

Binning trials into quartiles of each state and fitting a session-aware mixed
model made the two regimes explicit (RMSE regressed on condition, state and
their interaction, with random per-session intercepts and open/closed-loop
slopes and a mouse intercept; $n = 1670$ trials from 15 sessions and 4 mice, or
1190 trials from 11 sessions for motion; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}D). We summarise
each state by its effect on \emph{predictability}---the open-loop disturbance,
read from the trend of the uncontrolled error---and on \emph{controllability},
read from the trend of the open-minus-closed-loop gap, the condition-by-state
interaction. Predictability here is a property of the trial, not of the
stimulus response: it asks how well a fixed feedforward input anticipates what
that trial will do, whereas the prediction error of Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}G asks
how well the LTI model reproduces the response to the stimulus itself. The two
need not move together, and for motion they do not---movement trials are better
described by the impulse model and at the same time harder to hit open loop. Closed-loop control reduced error strongly at every state (gap at
mean state $p \le 3.7\times10^{-5}$ for all four factors), but the states differed in how
that benefit moved. Motion made the disturbance less predictable---open-loop
error rose with it ($p = 7.3\times10^{-4}$)---yet more controllable: the loop
rejected a growing share and the gap widened ($p = 0.0015$), the signature of
feedback rejecting an unanticipated disturbance. The same effect is visible
within sessions: regressing per-trial RMSE on concurrent motion energy, with
both quantities $z$-scored within session, open-loop error rose with motion
(median slope $+0.13$ standard deviations of RMSE per standard deviation of
motion; Wilcoxon signed-rank $p = 0.002$) while under closed-loop control the
dependence was abolished (median slope $-0.05$; $p = 0.47$), and the open-loop
slope exceeded the closed-loop slope in all 11 sessions ($p = 0.001$). Relative
2--4\,Hz power showed the opposite pattern: the open loop was unaffected while
closed-loop error climbed, so controllability fell ($+0.12$, 95\% CI
$[+0.01, +0.23]$, $p = 0.032$). The initial deviation and
absolute 1--4\,Hz power lowered predictability (open-loop error rose,
$p < 10^{-6}$) but left controllability unchanged---disturbances that scale both
conditions together. The controller therefore behaves as an internal model whose
rejection is steerable for the unanticipated motor disturbance but limited for
the ongoing low-frequency fluctuation, which is the component of brain state
that sets the irreducible closed-loop error.

\subsection*{Feedback rejects a state-dependent disturbance carried by the contralateral hemisphere}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Because the two hemispheres are strongly co-active, spontaneous activity in the
stimulated (ipsilateral) region is well predicted from the opposite hemisphere.
We fit a stimulation-blind linear predictor from contralateral pixels to the
ipsilateral control readout on spontaneous, no-laser epochs
(Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}E); it generalized to held-out pre-stimulus windows
(contralateral$\rightarrow$ipsilateral $R^2 = 0.85$ open loop and $0.89$ closed
loop; representative session), giving a trial-by-trial readout of the ongoing
brain state the controller must contend with.

We then used the predictor to separate the two sources at the single-trial
level. Applying it on every stimulation trial defines the \emph{Global}
component $G$---the activity the ipsilateral region would have shown with no
controller input, that is, the disturbance the controller faces on that
trial---and the \emph{Local} component $L = A - G$, what the laser and
controller added on top of it (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}F). This split is a
\emph{construction}, not a fitted model: it is the means by which a per-trial
disturbance becomes measurable at all, since $G$ is estimated without reference
to the laser. We therefore make no claim from the decomposition itself, and test
the controller against the disturbance it defines.

We then quantified how well closed-loop control rejects this disturbance with a
per-trial energy ratio $\mathrm{RR} = \|A - r\|^2 / \|D\|^2$: the residual energy 
of the actual response about the reference ($r = -5\%\,\Delta F/F$) divided by
the energy of the disturbance $D$---the contra-predicted excursion the controller
must cancel, referenced to its own no-laser baseline (leak-corrected)---over the
steady-state window (1--3\,s, after the inhibitory onset transient). Referencing
the disturbance to its own baseline rather than to the target keeps the fixed
setpoint offset from being counted as disturbance, so that $\mathrm{RR} < 1$
means the controlled response carries less energy than the disturbance it
counters. Closed-loop control rejected the disturbance across the population:
the geometric-mean ratio was $\mathrm{RR} = 0.58$ (95\% CI $[0.48, 0.71]$),
below one by a session-aware mixed model ($\log\mathrm{RR} \sim 1 +
(1|\mathrm{mouse}) + (1|\mathrm{mouse{:}session})$; one-sided
$p = 2.1\times10^{-8}$, $n = 11$ sessions; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}G). Two sessions
in which the controller never reached the setpoint---so the residual is
dominated by a standing setpoint error rather than by disturbance
rejection---were excluded.

\subsection*{Feedforward control for variable reference tracking}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Feedback and preview correct different failures of sinusoidal tracking: feedback removes trial-to-trial variability, while preview removes the phase lag imposed by the plant. We designed the controller to track a 1\,Hz sinusoidal reference for 4\,s. We compared four controller modes, open-loop (OL), open-loop with preview (OL$+$p), closed-loop (CL), and closed-loop with preview (CL$+$p). The preview block shifts the reference forward by a fixed lookahead, that is determined by open loop response characteristics, before it reaches the controller.  This controller compensates the delay of the cortical response rather than just its amplitude (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}A). We report one representative session in detail (200 sinusoidal trials; $n = 51, 46, 46, 57$ trials for OL, OL$+$p, CL, CL$+$p) and the consistency of each effect across three sessions.

Open-loop tracking followed the reference in shape but lagged it and drifted between trials (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}B). The trial average retained the sinusoid in every mode, with the across-trial spread widest under open loop (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}C); the trial-average laser commands that produced these responses are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}D.

Feedback reduced both the trial-to-trial spread and the error itself. Across-trial variance fell from $17.9$ to $14.8$ between OL and CL (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}E), and the time-resolved tracking error fell alongside it (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}F). Collapsing each trial to a single number, median RMSE fell from $3.82$ to $2.80$\,\%\,$\Delta F/F$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}G). Preview did not reproduce this effect: median RMSE changed little between OL and OL$+$p ($3.82$ versus $3.55$), and adding preview to closed-loop control did not improve it further (CL $2.80$ versus CL$+$p $3.30$). Closed-loop control with preview gave the tightest distribution of the four modes (SD $1.57$ versus $1.85$, $2.75$ and $2.19$ for OL, OL$+$p and CL) and reduced error relative to open loop (median $3.30$ versus $3.82$).

Preview specifically worked on lag compensation and improved tracking of a moving reference. The cortical response lagged the reference by $60^\circ$ under open loop and by $36^\circ$ under closed loop; adding preview moved both to approximately zero ($5^\circ$ and $-9^\circ$ respectively; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}H). The correction matched the lookahead by construction: a five-sample preview at 35\,Hz corresponds to $143$\,ms, or $51^\circ$ at the 1\,Hz drive frequency, and the measured open-loop correction across all pooled trials was $5.0$ samples ($51.4^\circ$, median).

All three effects held across sessions. Per-trial RMSE was lower under closed loop than open loop in 3/3 sessions (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}I), preview cancelled the lag in all three (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}J), and across-trial variance was lower under closed loop in 3/3 (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}K). Pooling per-trial RMSE across sessions showed the same reduction (mean $\pm$ SD: OL $4.58 \pm 1.77$, $n = 136$; CL $3.83 \pm 1.94$, $n = 100$) and no comparable change with preview (OL$+$p $4.47 \pm 2.22$, $n = 110$; CL$+$p $3.95 \pm 1.58$, $n = 132$). With three sessions we do not report significance tests; we treat the direction of each effect and its consistency across sessions as the result. The error reduction is therefore attributable to feedback and the lag correction to preview.

Decomposing the error clarifies why the two mechanisms do not overlap. Because the stimulation window contains exactly four cycles of the drive, per-trial squared error partitions without loss into a constant-offset term, a term at the drive frequency, and the remaining broadband term. Averaged over the three sessions, preview reduced the error at the drive frequency in both comparisons (OL $3.14 \rightarrow$ OL$+$p $0.89$, 3.5-fold; CL $1.78 \rightarrow$ CL$+$p $1.22$\,(\%\,$\Delta F/F)^2$, 1.5-fold), while feedback reduced the broadband term ($10.55 \rightarrow 6.62$ from OL to CL). Preview therefore acts on the component of error that a fixed phase relationship can remove, and feedback on the component generated by ongoing spontaneous activity. Closed-loop control with preview was the only mode to engage both, and had the lowest total error power in two of three sessions.
