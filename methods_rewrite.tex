\subsection*{Imaging and optogenetic stimulation}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

We imaged through the single-photon mesoscope of \citet{Matveev2024}, which
shares one light path between imaging and photostimulation. Blue (470\,nm) and
violet (405\,nm) illumination alternated frame by frame at 70\,Hz, giving 35\,Hz
per channel, over a $9.7 \times 9.7$\,mm field (17.3\,\textmu m per pixel),
with an exposure of 14\,ms per frame. Illumination was as in
\citet{Matveev2024}, where it did not detectably activate the opsin.
%% FILL: illumination power density at the brain surface (unknown; do not print a
%% value until measured).

The controller received only the blue frames and fed back on the blue-channel
signal without hemodynamic correction, which needs both channels and cannot be
done within one control interval. Offline, we removed hemodynamic signals by
regressing the violet- on the blue-channel signal, compressed the movies by
singular value decomposition and registered them to the atlas
\citep{Matveev2024}. The impulse analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}) used the
corrected signal. Every closed-loop analysis
(Figs.~\ref{fig:figure3}--\ref{fig:figure5}), including the band-power state
measures, used the uncorrected signal the controller fed back on (see
Discussion).

Stimulation used the 638\,nm galvanometer-steered laser of
\citet{Matveev2024}, delivered through the imaging objective. The target was
fixed within a session and placed adjacent to the readout site; suppression
spread beyond the stimulation spot (Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial_spread}A,B) and lowered
$\Delta F/F$ at the readout. Stimulation targets were about 2\,mm lateral and
1\,mm posterior to bregma ($(x, y) \approx (+2, -1)$\,mm), varying slightly
between mice. Laser power was calibrated at the focal plane once a month, as described by
\citet{Matveev2024}.

The controller computed its command in volts, so $K_r$ and $K_p$ are in V per
$\%\,\Delta F/F$ and $K_i$ in V per $\%\,\Delta F/F$ per s. Commands were
quantized to 0.01\,V and limited to 2.45\,V (in the first two sessions, the laser
input itself saturated at that level). We converted the recorded laser
modulation voltage to peak power at the focal plane with the laser calibration
\citep{Matveev2024} (1\,mW per 3\,V for the impulse and 2025 sessions; the
calibration nearest each session in 2026) and report all amplitudes in mW. Each
command set the laser power for one control sample (28.6\,ms). In a few early
sessions the laser was instead modulated by a 40\,Hz sine whose peak the
controller set at each sample, so mean power was half the reported peak.

\subsection*{Behavioral monitoring}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

An infrared camera filmed the face and forepaws during a subset of sessions,
synchronized with imaging as in \citet{Matveev2024}. We computed a scalar motion
energy signal per video frame as the squared pixel norm of the difference
between consecutive frames, and z-scored it within session.

\subsection*{Real-time control}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The control loop ran as a dedicated real-time process, woken by each blue frame
and kept separate from display and disk writing. End-to-end latency, from camera
exposure to stimulation command, was measured over 200 loop cycles
(Fig.~\ref{fig:figure1}F).

\emph{Online signal.} The feedback signal was the mean fluorescence in a
$10 \times 10$ pixel square (0.173\,mm on a side) centered on the readout site
in somatosensory cortex. With $F_t$ that regional mean at control sample $t$,
%
\begin{equation}
    y_{t} = 100 \times \frac{F_{t} - \bar{F}_{t}}{\bar{F}_{t}},
    \label{eq:dff}
\end{equation}
%
in percent, where $\bar{F}_{t}$ is the running mean of $F$ over the preceding
40.0\,s (1400 control samples at 35\,Hz). This length is the controller's
horizon parameter and was the same in all 15 sessions. No filtering was applied
to $y_t$. The baseline was frozen at trial onset and resumed updating at trial
end. Otherwise the evoked suppression would enter $\bar F$, compress $y_t$
toward zero over repeated trials, and change the meaning of the reference from
trial to trial. Offline, we re-extracted this signal from the compressed movie;
it matched the logged online signal ($r = 0.995$) and did not depend on region
size between $10 \times 10$ and $21 \times 21$ pixels.

\emph{Two $\Delta F/F$ normalizations.} Equation~\eqref{eq:dff} is the signal
the controller computed online, and every closed-loop analysis
(Figs.~\ref{fig:figure3}--\ref{fig:figure5}) uses it unchanged. The loop could
use only past samples, so re-normalizing these data by a retrospective
whole-session mean would describe a signal the controller never had. The
open-loop impulse experiments (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}) enter no feedback path
and are analyzed retrospectively, so they need no online baseline. They are
normalized by a fixed denominator, $\Delta F/F = 100 \times F /
\bar{F}_{\mathrm{img}}$, where $\bar{F}_{\mathrm{img}}$ is the session mean image
averaged over the readout kernel. The state analysis of Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}G
is invariant to any constant rescaling of $\Delta F/F$ (its quartiles are
rank-based and its dependent variable is scaled within amplitude), so it does
not depend on this choice.

\emph{Trial structure.} Stimulation was delivered in 3\,s trials. Inter-trial
intervals were 15\,s plus a uniform random jitter of up to 1\,s, so that laser
onset times were not predictable and did not entrain adaptation; no light was
applied between trials. The reference was held at
$y_{\mathrm{ref}} = -5\%\,\Delta F/F$ throughout the stimulation window, a
suppression of five percentage points below the running baseline. Each session
began with a tuning phase (\nameref{sec:gain_opt}), followed by a validation
block of 30--200 trials (Table~\ref{tab:gains}). On each validation trial the
condition was drawn independently with probability one half, so open- and
closed-loop trials were interleaved in random order, balanced only
approximately, and sampled the same distribution of brain states. In Mice 3
and 4 the tuning phase swept $K_p$, and only the final 100 trials, with
$K_p$ held fixed, entered the comparison. Sessions lasted about one hour.

\subsection*{Controller}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection*{Nominal model and trial averaging}

The cortical response at the readout site is nonlinear and depends on ongoing
brain state, which we cannot measure. We write the underlying system as
%
\begin{align}
    x_{t+1} &= f(x_{t},\,u_{t},\,\rho_{t}) + d_{t}, \label{eq:nl_state}\\
    y_{t}   &= h(x_{t},\,u_{t},\,\rho_{t}) + v_{t}, \label{eq:nl_out}
\end{align}
%
where $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is a latent cortical state, $u_t \in \mathbb{R}$
the scalar optogenetic input, $\rho_t$ an unmeasured brain-state variable
(arousal, movement, cortical synchrony), $d_t$ an unobserved input from other
regions, $y_t$ the measured $\Delta F/F$ of \eqref{eq:dff}, and $v_t$
measurement noise. The functions $f$ and $h$ are unknown.

Because $\rho_t$ is unmeasured, and because physiological limits on input
amplitude, stimulus duration and trial count rule out frequency sweeps,
persistent excitation and subspace identification, we did not identify
\eqref{eq:nl_state}--\eqref{eq:nl_out}. We designed instead against a nominal
linear time-invariant model of the trial-averaged response,
%
\begin{align}
    x_{t+1} &= \bar{A}\,x_{t} + \bar{B}\,u_{t}, \label{eq:lti_state}\\
    y_{t}   &= C\,x_{t},                        \label{eq:lti_out}
\end{align}
%
in which $C$ is the fixed spatial averaging kernel of \eqref{eq:dff} and
$(\bar A, \bar B)$ are the trial-averaged dynamics.

\begin{assumption}[Trial averaging]
\label{assm:trial_avg}
Averaged over initial conditions, disturbances, and brain states, the output of
\eqref{eq:nl_state}--\eqref{eq:nl_out} is well approximated by the output of the
nominal system \eqref{eq:lti_state}--\eqref{eq:lti_out} driven by the same
input:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}_{x_{0},\,d,\,\rho}\!\left[Y_{0:T}\right]
    \;\approx\; \bar{Y}_{0:T}\!\left(\bar{x}_{0},\,U_{0:T-1}\right),
    \qquad \bar{x}_{0} = \mathbb{E}[x_{0}],
    \label{eq:trial_avg}
\end{equation}
where $Y_{0:T}$ is the output trajectory of the true system under input
$U_{0:T-1}$ and $\bar{Y}_{0:T}$ that of the nominal system. We take $x_0$, $d$,
and $\rho$ to be drawn from stationary distributions with
$\mathbb{E}[d_t] = 0$.
\end{assumption}

Equation~\eqref{eq:trial_avg} is an approximation, since
$\mathbb{E}[f(x)] \neq f(\mathbb{E}[x])$ for nonlinear $f$; it asserts that the
residual is small and close enough to zero mean for a controller designed on
$(\bar A, \bar B, C)$ to work. Trial-to-trial departures from the averaged
response are then treated as zero-mean disturbances. Two observations support
it. First, the cross-trial
variance of spontaneous $\Delta F/F$ is estimated consistently: its mean is flat
in trial count to within 1--2\%, while its SEM falls roughly as $N^{-1/2}$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial_spread}C). Second, the stimulus does not inflate
across-trial variance: in open-loop trials it falls during stimulation and
recovers afterwards (Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial_spread}D).

\subsubsection*{Tracking error and feedforward term}

Light output is positive only and stimulation suppresses activity, so the
command must rise when activity is above a negative reference. The tracking
error is
%
\begin{equation}
    e_{t} = y_{t} - y_{\mathrm{ref}} = y,
    \label{eq:error}
\end{equation}
%
so $e_t > 0$ means the cortex is not yet suppressed enough and the controller
raises the command. The feedforward term supplies the nominal command needed to
bring the output near the reference. It is constant over the stimulation window
and does not depend on the measurement:
%
\begin{equation}
    \bar{u} = K_{r}\,\lvert y_{\mathrm{ref}} \rvert .
    \label{eq:ff}
\end{equation}
%
We estimated $K_{r}$ by least squares from the impulse-response experiments. Let
$U = [u_{1},\ldots,u_{K}]$ be the stimulus amplitudes and
$Y = [\,y_{1} - y_{\mathrm{ref}},\ldots,y_{K} - y_{\mathrm{ref}}\,]$ the
corresponding trial-averaged output deviations from the reference. Then
%
\begin{equation}
    K_{r} = U\,Y^{\dagger},
    \label{eq:kr}
\end{equation}
%
where $Y^{\dagger}$ is the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse. This regresses amplitude
on measured output, the direction an actuator command needs, and so is not the
reciprocal of the forward dose--response slope. $K_r$ was then adjusted by hand
at the start of each session (\nameref{sec:gain_opt}). Open- and closed-loop
trials within a session share the same $K_r$, so the comparison between them
does not depend on its value.

\subsubsection*{Proportional--integral feedback}
\label{sec:pi_controller_design}

Feedforward alone cannot correct bias in $K_r$, residual nonlinearity, or
state-dependent trial-to-trial deviation, so we added proportional and integral
feedback on the error of \eqref{eq:error}. The command at sample $t$ was
%
\begin{equation}
    u_{t} = \mathrm{sat}_{[0,\,u_{\max}]}
            \Big(\bar{u} + g\,\big(u^{P}_{t} + u^{I}_{t}\big)\Big),
    \qquad
    u^{P}_{t} = \mathrm{sat}_{[-p,\,p]}\!\left(K_{p}\,e_{t}\right),
    \qquad
    u^{I}_{t} = \mathrm{sat}_{[-q,\,q]}\!\left(K_{i}\,\bar{e}_{t}\right),
    \label{eq:pi}
\end{equation}
%
where $g = 1$ on closed-loop trials and $g = 0$ on open-loop trials, and
$u_{\max} = 2.45$\,V. The proportional term was clamped at $p = 5$\,V, above
$u_{\max}$, and the integral term at $q = 1$\,V. The integral is a trailing
boxcar rather than an unbounded sum,
%
\begin{equation}
    \bar{e}_{t} = \frac{1}{f_{s}}\sum_{k = t - M + 1}^{t} e_{k},
    \qquad M = 105 \ \text{samples} \ (3\,\text{s at } f_s = 35\,\text{Hz}),
    \label{eq:integral}
\end{equation}
%
which spans the whole 3\,s trial. It was reset during every inter-trial
interval, so it can never hold more than 3\,s of error and no error carries
into the next trial. On open-loop trials ($g = 0$) the constant $\bar u$ of
\eqref{eq:ff} is the only input: no measurement enters the command, so the
baseline is open loop in the strict sense. The most power the laser could
deliver at $u_{\max}$ was about 1\,mW at the focal plane in most 2025 sessions
(1.6\,mW in the first two).

Gains and trial counts for every session are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:gains}. No session showed oscillatory instability or divergence
at these gains. We therefore treat the closed loop as stable over the operating
range used here, rather than certifying gain and phase margins: with a
saturating, one-sided actuator and a finite-window integral, margins from a
linear nominal model would describe a controller other than the one that ran.

The structure of the controller nonetheless rests on a standard result for the nominal, trial-averaged plant.

\begin{rem}[Low-gain integral tracking through a monotone actuator]
\label{rem:lowgain}
Write the nominal plant from drive to suppression $s = -y$ as a linear time-invariant system $G$ driven through a static nonlinearity $\phi$ of the integrator state $u$. With $K_p = 0$ and $u = K_i\bar e$, the controller of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pi} is the loop below with $k = K_i$, provided $\phi$ absorbs the feedforward offset $\bar u$, the clamp of the integral term to $[-q, q]$, the clipping of the command to $[0, u_{\max}]$ and the relation between command and effective drive. Suppose that (i) $G$ is exponentially stable, possibly with an input delay; (ii) its steady-state gain is positive, $G(0) > 0$; (iii) $\phi$ is nondecreasing and globally Lipschitz with constant $\lambda$ (we ignore the 0.01\,V quantization of the command, a staircase that is not Lipschitz); and (iv) the reference is feasible, that is, $|y_{\mathrm{ref}}|/G(0)$ lies in the range of $\phi$, which with the clamps above is the drive delivered by commands between $\max(0, \bar u - q)$ and $\min(u_{\max}, \bar u + q)$; if the unstimulated output carries a constant offset $y_0$, $|y_{\mathrm{ref}}|$ is replaced by $|y_{\mathrm{ref}}| + y_0$. Then there is a $K > 0$, depending only on $G$, such that for every integral gain $0 < k < K/\lambda$ the integral loop $\dot{u} = k\,e$, with $e = |y_{\mathrm{ref}}| - s = y - y_{\mathrm{ref}}$ as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:error}, tracks the reference: $\phi(u(t)) \to |y_{\mathrm{ref}}|/G(0)$, $u(t)$ converges, the error is square-integrable, and $e(t) \to 0$ under a regularity condition that is mild for a finite-dimensional plant with input delay \citep[Theorem~4.1(a),(c),(d),(f) and Remark~4.2(b)]{logemann2000absolute}. A gain below which tracking is guaranteed, that is, a lower bound on $K/\lambda$, can be computed from open-loop step- or frequency-response data \citep{logemann1999integral}; analogous results hold in discrete time \citep{fliegner2001discrete} and, for proportional--integral control, for nonlinear plants whose steady-state input--output map is an increasing bijection with slope bounded away from zero, a condition that a saturating actuator violates \citep{desoer1985tracking}.
\end{rem}

The trial-averaged impulse-response models (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}), recorded in separate sessions and in two of the four controller mice, are stable, satisfying (i). They do not by themselves fix the sign of $G(0)$, the integral of the impulse response over all time, because their slowest mode exceeded the 0.5\,s fit window in three of four sessions. That the response keeps its suppressive sign over a trial, which is what (ii) requires on our time scale, is shown instead by the open-loop trials, in which a constant command held activity below baseline for the whole trial (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}B). Evoked suppression grew monotonically with laser power (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}B); although it is a transient measure, it makes the steady-state map monotone through the structure of the nominal model, a static actuator map feeding linear dynamics, given (ii). The clipping, the clamp on the integral term and this monotone dose--response make $\phi$ nondecreasing, satisfying (iii), and (iv) requires the reference, plus any standing offset of the unstimulated activity, to lie within the suppression attainable with the commands the clamps allow. The controller that ran departs from the remark in four further ways: it had a proportional term, its integral was a 3\,s trailing window rather than an unbounded sum (Eq.~\eqref{eq:integral}), it ran in discrete time at 35\,Hz, and each trial lasted 3\,s (4\,s with the sinusoidal reference) with the integrator reset between trials, whereas the remark is asymptotic and concerns the nominal plant, not one that varies with brain state. The remark therefore motivates the controller's structure rather than certifying the loop that ran. That loop's stability rests on the observation that no session showed sustained oscillation; with a stable plant and a bounded command the output cannot diverge, so oscillation is the instability the data could have shown.

\subsubsection*{Sinusoidal reference and preview}

For Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5} the reference varied in time:
%
\begin{equation}
    r_{k} = A\left[1 - \cos\!\left(2\pi f\,k / f_{s}\right)\right],
    \qquad y_{\mathrm{ref},k} = -\tilde{r}_{k},
    \label{eq:sine_ref}
\end{equation}
%
with $f = 1$\,Hz, so the setpoint swings between 0 and $-2A\%\,\Delta F/F$ and
the 4\,s stimulation window holds exactly four cycles. Preview acts on the
reference before it reaches the controller,
%
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{r}_{k} = r_{k} + K_{v}\,(r_{k+1} - r_{k})
                + K_{a}\,(r_{k+1} - 2r_{k} + r_{k-1})
                + K_{\mathrm{pv}}\,(r_{k+d} - r_{k}),
    \label{eq:preview}
\end{equation}
%
and the previewed setpoint replaces $y_{\mathrm{ref}}$ in both the feedforward
term and the error: $\bar{u}_{k} = K_{r}\,\tilde{r}_{k}$ and
$e_{k} = y_{k} + \tilde{r}_{k}$. In every session $K_{v} = K_{a} = 0$ and
$K_{\mathrm{pv}} = 1$, so $\tilde{r}_{k} = r_{k+d}$, a pure lookahead of
$d = 5$ samples (143\,ms, or $51.4^{\circ}$ at 1\,Hz), chosen to match the lag
of the open-loop response. Each trial was assigned independently and uniformly
at random to one of four modes: open loop (OL; $g = 0$, $K_{\mathrm{pv}} = 0$),
open loop with preview (OL$+$p; $g = 0$, $K_{\mathrm{pv}} = 1$), closed loop (CL;
$g = 1$, $K_{\mathrm{pv}} = 0$) and closed loop with preview (CL$+$p; $g = 1$,
$K_{\mathrm{pv}} = 1$). Three sessions from one mouse contributed
(Table~\ref{tab:cohort}).

All sinusoidal metrics are computed from stimulation onset to $+4$\,s against
the logged reference. Per-trial RMSE is as in the static case
(Eq.~\ref{eq:trial_cost}). Across-trial variance is the variance across trials
at each sample, averaged over the window. Phase lag is the difference between
the phases of least-squares fits of $b_{0} + b_{1}\sin(2\pi f t) + b_{2}\cos(2\pi
f t)$ to the trial-averaged response and to the reference. Per-trial lag is the
lag of the cross-correlation peak between response and reference, searched
within half a period. Because the window holds an integer number of cycles,
1\,Hz falls on a discrete Fourier bin, and Parseval's theorem partitions each
trial's mean squared error exactly into a constant offset, the 1\,Hz component
and a broadband remainder. These are averaged within session and then across
sessions.

\subsection*{Model-predictive control replay}
\label{sec:mpc}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

We simulated a model-predictive controller (MPC) on one closed-loop session
(Mouse~1, session m4; 108 closed-loop trials). Activity at the controlled site
and at 100 sites over dorsal cortex, with the laser command as input, was
modeled as a linear vector autoregressive process with exogenous input (VARX;
35 lags, 1\,s, of every channel and of the laser command; construction and
held-out prediction in Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_varx}). The 100 sites were an evenly
spaced grid of $5 \times 5$ pixel boxes over the brain mask. The VARX was fitted
by ridge regression; the penalty (100) and the history length were checked on
validation data over $10$--$10^{4}$ and 5--35 frames, where prediction was nearly
flat (Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_varx}D). At
every frame the MPC chose the laser commands over a 1\,s horizon that minimized
the squared deviation of the predicted controlled signal from the reference,
plus a penalty ($\lambda = 0.1$) on command changes and a small penalty pulling
the command toward the median recorded closed-loop command. Commands were
bounded between 0 and the largest command the rig delivered. The MPC applied
the first command and planned again at the next frame.

To replay a trial, we drove the VARX fitted to the whole session with that
trial's recorded one-step residuals. Under the recorded laser this reproduces
the recorded trial exactly, so the PI baseline is the trial as it ran and needs
no model of the PI loop. The MPC's own model never saw closed-loop data: it was
fitted on the session's spontaneous and open-loop frames only, so no closed-loop
trial, including the one being replayed, entered its training. RMSE was scored
over the steady-state window (1--3\,s).
We repeated the replay on the CL$+$p trials of the sinusoidal session
of Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5} (Mouse~3), with the 1\,Hz reference in the MPC cost over its horizon,
and scored RMSE over the 4\,s window. Here too the MPC's model was fitted without
any closed-loop (CL or CL$+$p) trial, on spontaneous and open-loop frames, with the
replayed trial's fold (one of five) also held out. All MPC results are descriptive.

\subsection*{System characterization}

\subsubsection*{Impulse responses and evoked suppression}
\label{sec:inhib_energy}

Impulse experiments were run open loop with the same imaging. Each stimulus was
a single pulse about one imaging frame long (23--28\,ms), separated by
randomized intervals (median 5.7--9.8\,s across sessions). Responses were read
out offline from the hemodynamically corrected movie over the readout region,
normalized as in \emph{Two $\Delta F/F$ normalizations}. To test whether the
input--output relation was linear and monotonic, we delivered stimuli at three
to nine nonzero amplitudes per session, up to 1.6\,mW, in pseudorandom order.
Every nonzero amplitude had at least 44 trials.

We summarized each response by its mean $\Delta F/F$ over the 200\,ms after
onset (evoked suppression),
%
\begin{equation}
    E_{j} = \frac{1}{\lvert W \rvert}\sum_{t \in W} y_{j,t},
    \qquad W = \{t_{\mathrm{on}},\ldots,t_{\mathrm{on}}+7\},
    \label{eq:inhib_energy}
\end{equation}
%
where $j$ indexes trials and $W$ spans the eight samples from onset to 200\,ms.
Each trial's trace is referenced to its value at the onset sample, so
$y_{j,t_{\mathrm{on}}} = 0$. We use the window mean because it is less
sensitive to single-sample noise than the trough. We regressed mean evoked
suppression on amplitude to test linearity.

\emph{Spatial extent.} We mapped the trial-averaged $\Delta F/F$ across the
imaged cortex at each amplitude and counted pixels whose background-subtracted
response fell below 50\% of the median response within 0.2\,mm of the peak at
the strongest amplitude. Background was estimated per amplitude from an annulus
0.6--0.9\,mm from the peak, which removes the global hemodynamic component.
Areas use the 17.3\,\textmu m pixel size (Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial_spread}A,B).

\subsubsection*{Transfer-function fitting}
\label{sec:tf_fitting}

We fitted a continuous-time transfer function to each session's
amplitude-normalized impulse response. The trial-averaged response at each
amplitude $a$ was divided by $a$, and these normalized responses were averaged
with weights proportional to $a^{2}$, so that the high signal-to-noise
amplitudes set the dynamics, giving $h(t)$ over the 0.5\,s after onset. Under
linearity the normalized responses coincide, so the fit itself tests the linear
assumption. We fitted $h(t)$ by prediction-error minimization, sweeping 1--5
poles, 0--4 zeros (strictly proper, so $h(0) = 0$) and 0--3 samples of pure
delay (0--85.7\,ms), and selected the order by the Akaike Information Criterion.
When the selected model reproduced $h(t)$ with $R^{2} < 0.98$, we instead took
the model with the fewest parameters among those within 0.002 of the best
$R^{2}$. In one session (Mouse 3), whose onset-referenced response stayed offset
from zero, no order fitted well from the default initialization (best
$R^2 = 0.94$). We therefore added, in every session, one candidate initialized
from a fit to the baseline-referenced response plus one pole near the origin.
It was selected only in Mouse 3 (five poles, four zeros, $R^{2} = 0.99$). The
near-origin pole contributes less than 0.2\% of the response and is not
interpreted. Held-out refits rebuilt this initialization from training trials
only.

\emph{Held-out validation} (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}C,D). For each of 100 random
splits, trials were divided in half within each amplitude, so that both halves
span the full dose range. We rebuilt $h(t)$ on one half, refitted the model at
the session's selected order, and scored its prediction against the other half,
in both directions (200 folds). The prediction carries the model's own gain, so
a gain mismatch counts against it. We report the median and interquartile range
of held-out $R^{2}$ over the folds whose refit succeeded (90\% in Mouse 3, at
least 98\% elsewhere); the pooled value is the median over all such folds of the
four sessions. Model order was selected once on all trials and is not itself
cross-validated.

\emph{Cross-session model swap} (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}E). Entry $(i,j)$ is the
$R^{2}$ of session $i$'s fitted unit response against session $j$'s measured
$h(t)$, after a single least-squares gain for that pair. The free gain restricts
the comparison to the shape of the response, which a controller cannot re-tune.
The diagonal is each model's in-sample fit.

\emph{Time constants} (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}F). Each continuous-time pole $p$
gives $\tau = -1/\mathrm{Re}(p)$. We report the fastest (dip) and slowest
(rebound) modes per session. A time constant longer than the 0.5\,s fit window
is not resolved by the fit and is drawn at the window edge. To check
identifiability, we refitted 300 bootstrap resamples of trials within amplitude,
each rebuilding $h(t)$ and refitting at the selected order, and computed the
fraction of refits whose slowest $\tau$ exceeded the fit window (18 samples,
0--0.49\,s), counting failed refits (fewer than 3\%) as not exceeding it.
Because this fraction was large in three of four sessions (Results), we show no
intervals.

\subsection*{Controller tuning}
\label{sec:gain_opt}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

We scored tracking by the root-mean-squared deviation from the reference. For
trial $i$, with the errors over the evaluation window collected in
$e_{i} \in \mathbb{R}^{T}$,
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RMSE}_{i} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\,\lVert e_{i} \rVert_{2},
    \qquad
    \hat{J}(K) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{RMSE}_{i},
    \label{eq:trial_cost}
\end{equation}
%
so values carry the units of $\Delta F/F$, and the cost of a gain pair $K$ is the
mean of per-trial RMSE over its $N$ trials.

We used three named evaluation windows. The \textbf{full stimulation window},
$t = 0$ to $+3$\,s, was used for per-trial and cross-session RMSE. Within it,
the \textbf{transient window}, $t = 0$ to $+1$\,s, covers the approach to the
reference after the laser-evoked inhibitory deflection, and the
\textbf{steady-state window}, $t = +1$ to $+3$\,s, the interval over which the
controller holds the settled response. The 1\,s boundary is a fixed convention,
about five times the $\sim$200\,ms impulse dip. The steady-state window alone
was used for disturbance rejection and for every state analysis of
Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4} (panels D and G; panel C reports both windows), because
it excludes the laser-evoked transient.

\emph{Gain grid.} Gradients of the cost require a model, so we mapped the cost
surface directly. In one session of Mouse~1 the controller cycled
through 10 fixed PI gain pairs, and we estimated $\hat{J}(K)$ at each node over
the full window (Fig.~\ref{fig:cost_landscape}A,D). The minimum fell at
$(K_p, K_i) = (0.05,\,0.10)$, with $\hat{J} = 16.1$. We read the grid as showing
a smooth cost surface with a low-cost basin, not as locating a global optimum.

\emph{Online gain search.} In a second session of the same mouse
(136 trials), the controller tuned its own gains during the
experiment with the zeroth-order search of Algorithm~\ref{alg:zo}, starting from
open loop ($K_p = K_i = 0$). A candidate replaced the current gains only if it
lowered the running best cost. The online cost was evaluated over the
stimulation window less two samples, so its values are not comparable with the
grid. Two candidates were accepted, lowering the online cost from 16.6 to 12.3,
and the search ended at $(K_p, K_i) = (0.07,\,0.06)$, inside the low-cost region
of the grid (Fig.~\ref{fig:cost_landscape}A--C). We report this run as a
feasibility demonstration, not as a reliable optimizer.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Zeroth-order controller gain search}
\label{alg:zo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Initialize:} $K_{\text{best}} = [0,\,0]$; step
       $\mathbf{r} = [0.1,\,0.01]$; anneal $\gamma = 0.8$; $N = 15$ trials;
       $I = 9$ iterations
\For{$i = 1$ \textbf{to} $I$}
    \State Sample a unit direction $\mathbf{v}$ uniformly on the circle
    \State Propose $K \leftarrow K_{\text{best}} + \mathbf{r}\odot\mathbf{v}$
    \For{$j = 1$ \textbf{to} $N$}
        \State Run one trial under $K$ and record $\mathrm{RMSE}_j$
    \EndFor
    \State $\hat{J}(K) \leftarrow \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{RMSE}_j$
    \If{$\hat{J}(K) < \hat{J}(K_{\text{best}})$}
        \State $K_{\text{best}} \leftarrow K$
    \EndIf
    \State $\mathbf{r} \leftarrow \gamma\,\mathbf{r}$
\EndFor
\State \textbf{return} $K_{\text{best}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\emph{Gains used for validation.} For each mouse, initial gains were set by hand
within the range the grid had explored, starting from the least-squares $K_r$
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:kr}. Later sessions started from the gains of earlier sessions,
with small adjustments at the start of each session. Gains were then held fixed
for the validation block (Table~\ref{tab:gains}).

\subsection*{Session and trial selection}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

All exclusions are collected here.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Motion.} No trial was excluded on motion. Motion enters the
          analyses only as a state variable.
    \item \emph{Behavioral video.} Motion analyses used the 11 of 15 sessions
          with a usable motion trace (1190 trials).
    \item \emph{Predictor availability.} The disturbance analysis
          (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}E--G) needs a contralateral predictor, which
          could be fitted in 13 of the 15 sessions; two sessions lacked a usable
          full-frame recording aligned to the control readout. No session was
          excluded on predictor quality; we report the predictor's held-out
          performance instead.
    \item \emph{Standing setpoint offset.} For the disturbance-rejection analysis
          only, we excluded sessions whose closed-loop response stayed above the
          reference throughout stimulation, because the rejection ratio would
          count that standing offset as unrejected disturbance. Over the
          steady-state window the residual energy splits exactly into
          $\overline{(A-r)^{2}} = (\bar{A}-r)^{2} + \mathrm{var}(A)$, and we
          excluded a session when the first term, the standing offset, supplied
          more than one third of the total. This removed sessions m2 (Mouse~1) and
          m15 (Mouse~4; Table~\ref{tab:gains}). We chose the threshold after inspecting the
          sessions; any threshold between 20\% and 37\% selects the same eleven.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Brain-state measures}
\label{sec:disturbance}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

We characterized cortical state on each trial with up to four quantities. The
impulse analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}G) and the controller analysis
(Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}) measure them over different windows, deliberately,
because they ask different questions.

The \emph{initial deviation} (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4} only) is the absolute
distance of the readout from the reference at the onset sample,
$\lvert y(t_{\mathrm{on}}) - r \rvert$, with $r = -5\%\,\Delta F/F$. It measures
how far from target a trial starts. \emph{Motion energy} is the mean over the
window of the z-scored motion signal (Behavioral monitoring): the mean, not the
mean square, of the z-score, because the square is smallest for trials near the
session mean and would rank unusually still trials alongside active ones.
\emph{Relative 2--4\,Hz power} is the ratio of band power in 2--4\,Hz, the
sub-band least attenuated by the controller, to total power in 0.4--10\,Hz. The
ratio avoids the confound that any absolute spectral measure co-varies with the
overall magnitude of the signal, which differs between conditions by
construction. \emph{Absolute low-frequency power} is band power without that
normalization: 2--4\,Hz in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}G and $\log_{10}$ of 1--4\,Hz
power in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}. We report it alongside the relative measure
because a factor that acts through the amplitude of ongoing activity shows in the
absolute measure only.

\emph{Windows.} In Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}G, motion, relative and absolute power
are taken over the same strictly pre-stimulus window, $[-1, 0)$\,s, the 35
samples before onset. An impulse is a single brief input, so the question there
is how the state in which the stimulus arrives alters its response, and a
pre-stimulus window measures that state without overlapping the 0--200\,ms
response. In Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4} all three are taken over one common window,
$[-2, +3)$\,s, the 175 samples from 2\,s before onset to the end of
stimulation, because tracking performance depends both on the state a trial
begins in and on activity that develops while the controller acts. These
measures therefore describe concurrent activity rather than a pre-stimulus
state; the effect of feedback is assessed through the open- versus closed-loop
contrast, in which both conditions are measured identically.

\emph{Spectral estimation.} In Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}G each 35-sample segment was
mean-subtracted and Hann-tapered, and band power was the sum of the one-sided
periodogram over the native 1\,Hz bins inside the band, edges inclusive. In
Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4} each 175-sample segment $x$ was linearly detrended,
Hann-tapered with window $w$ and zero-padded to at least 8192 points, giving the
one-sided spectral density
%
\begin{equation}
    S(f) = \frac{2\,\lvert X(f) \rvert^{2}}{f_{s} \sum_{n} w_{n}^{2}},
    \qquad
    P_{[f_1, f_2]} = \int_{f_1}^{f_2} S(f)\,df ,
    \label{eq:bandpow}
\end{equation}
%
with the integral evaluated by the trapezoid rule between the exact band edges.
Integrating between exact edges makes the measure independent of segment length
(Spearman $\rho = 0.9999$ across 174-, 175- and 176-sample windows), which
summing native bins is not: at 0.2\,Hz resolution a one-sample change moves the
nominal band edges.

\emph{Quartile display} (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}D). We binned each session's
trials into quartiles of that session's state distribution (open- and
closed-loop trials together), z-scored per-trial RMSE within session using the
pooled open- and closed-loop distribution, averaged it within each quartile and
condition, and averaged these session means across sessions (mean $\pm$ SEM).
Pooling the conditions for the z-score keeps the level difference between them
while removing session-to-session differences in scale. The test of state
dependence is the mixed model under \nameref{sec:stats}.

\subsection*{State exemplars and error decomposition}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

To ask which state accounts for closed-loop tracking error, and when within the
trial, we analyzed the closed-loop trials of the 11 sessions with motion capture
(613 trials; 16 to 108 per session) one session at a time. The outcome was
per-trial RMSE in the transient and steady-state windows. The four state
measures were z-scored within session, with absolute power entered as its
logarithm. For each session and window we fitted ordinary least-squares models
with an intercept and report each factor's \emph{unique} $R^2$, the drop in
adjusted $R^2$ when that factor is removed,
%
\begin{equation}
    R^{2}_{\mathrm{unique},j} = \bar{R}^{2}(\mathcal{M}) - \bar{R}^{2}(\mathcal{M} \setminus j),
    \qquad \bar{R}^{2} = 1 - (1 - R^{2})\,\frac{n - 1}{n - p - 1},
    \label{eq:uniqueR2}
\end{equation}
%
for a model with $p$ factors fitted to $n$ trials. Adjusted $R^2$ removes the
positive floor that in-sample $R^2$ differences have at these trial counts.
Relative and absolute power are correlated, and in one four-factor model one
would absorb the variance the other shares with it. In Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}C
each power measure therefore enters its own three-factor model on the same base,
$\{\text{initial deviation}, \text{motion}, \text{relative}\}$ and
$\{\text{initial deviation}, \text{motion}, \text{absolute}\}$; the unique $R^2$
of initial deviation and motion are taken from the first. We report the mean and
SEM over sessions. Fig.~\ref{fig:f4_exemplars}B--D repeats the decomposition
with both power terms in one model, and with each alone.

The exemplar trials (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}A) illustrate each state as cleanly as
the data allow. With $z_{ij}$ the value of state $j$ on trial $i$, z-scored over
the trials from which the exemplar is drawn, the exemplar for state $j$ is the
trial that maximizes $z_{ij} - \max_{k \neq j} z_{ik}$ among trials with
$z_{ij} > 1$ (or, if none exceeds 1, the trial with the largest $z_{ij}$).
Exemplars are drawn from the pool for Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}A and within each
session for Fig.~\ref{fig:f4_exemplars}A.

\subsection*{Impulse-response variability and brain state}
\label{sec:imp_state}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

For Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}G we asked whether brain state changes how far a
single impulse response departs from the trial average. Any fixed
amplitude-to-response model, the LTI model included, predicts the same response
for every trial at a given amplitude, so we took as each trial's variability the
root-mean-square distance between its response and the trial-averaged response
at its amplitude,
%
\begin{equation}
e_{j} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t} \bigl(x_{j}(t) - \bar{x}_{s,a}(t)\bigr)^{2}},
\label{eq:l2_error}
\end{equation}
%
for trial $j$ at amplitude $a$ in session $s$, where $x_{j}(t)$ is the
onset-referenced trace and the sum runs over the $N = 18$ samples from 0 to
0.49\,s, the LTI fit window. Unlike the evoked suppression
(Eq.~\ref{eq:inhib_energy}), $e_{j}$ counts a dip that arrives early or late, or
a rebound of the wrong size, as error. Only nonzero amplitudes were analyzed
(1516 trials in four sessions). To pool amplitudes we scaled the error by its
within-amplitude root mean square, $z_{j} = e_{j} / \mathrm{RMS}_{s,a}(e)$.

Each state measure ($[-1, 0)$\,s window) was converted to a within-session
percentile, and trials were binned into quartiles of that percentile. We
summarize the pattern as the ratio of variability in the top to the bottom
quartile, computed in each session and reported as the geometric mean of the
four per-session ratios with their range; with four sessions we attach no
confidence intervals. This analysis is descriptive: it sets up the pattern that
the closed-loop experiments then test (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}), and the Results
draw no inference from it. For completeness, Table~\ref{tab:stat_fig2g} lists a
supporting test: within each session, the ordinary least-squares slope of $z$ on
state (standardized within session), and a one-sample $t$-test of the four
slopes against zero (three degrees of freedom). Two of the four sessions come
from one mouse. Pooled Spearman correlations are effect sizes only, without
$p$-values, because trials within a session are not independent.

\emph{Stimulus-free window.} We repeated the quartile ratio and the pooled
Spearman correlation on a matched quantity that contains no stimulus:
Eq.~\eqref{eq:l2_error} over the 18 samples from $-1.51$ to $-1.03$\,s,
referenced to the first sample of that window and compared with that window's
mean trace at the same amplitude, then scaled in the same way. This window lies
before the state window, and the response window does not overlap it. A state
that changes the spread of this control as much as that of the response acts on
the amplitude of ongoing activity rather than on the response to the stimulus.
Fig.~\ref{fig:state_quartile} divides each session's response variability by its
stimulus-free variability in every quartile, a ratio that stays flat for such a
state, and shows the single-trial errors. It also shows single trials sorted by
state at one amplitude per session (each session's second-highest), in raw
$\Delta F/F$. Its supporting test (Table~\ref{tab:stat_fig2g}) compares the top quartile of this
ratio with the mean of the other three (one-sample $t$-test on the four
per-session log ratios, three degrees of freedom).

\subsection*{Contralateral prediction and disturbance rejection}
\label{sec:disturbance_decomp}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

On spontaneous, no-laser frames we fitted a ridge-regularized linear predictor
from contralateral pixels to the ipsilateral readout. Predictors were about 500
pixels on a uniform lattice over the contralateral cortex, each z-scored on the
training data. Spontaneous frames were split in time, the first two thirds for
training and the last third for testing, and we report the held-out $R^{2}$.
The ridge penalty was chosen without stimulation trials: it was the smallest
penalty at which the predictor's spurious deflection on 200 laser-free catch
windows (trial-shaped windows baselined like a real trial) stopped falling.
Held-out $R^2$ peaks near zero penalty and cannot set it.

On each stimulation trial we applied the fixed predictor to concurrent
contralateral activity. This gives the \emph{Global} component $G(t)$, an
estimate of the ipsilateral activity expected with no controller input, and the
\emph{Local} component $L(t) = A(t) - G(t)$, where $A(t)$ is the measured
response. We checked the decomposition by reconstructing $A$ as $G$ plus the
leave-one-trial-out average local response, and report the cross-validated
$R^{2}$ of that reconstruction over 0--3\,s.

We quantified rejection per trial as the energy ratio
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RR} = \frac{\lVert A - r \rVert^{2}}{\lVert D \rVert^{2}},
    \label{eq:rr}
\end{equation}
%
where the numerator is the energy of the measured response about the reference
$r = -5\%\,\Delta F/F$ and the denominator the energy of the disturbance $D$, the
contralaterally predicted excursion referenced to its own no-laser baseline and
leak-corrected. Contralateral activity also changes during stimulation, so $G$
dips with the laser. The leak correction subtracts, from each trial's $G$
referenced to the 1\,s before onset, the session's mean open-loop $G$ over the
same window, so that $D$ keeps only the trial-to-trial excursion. Both terms
were evaluated over the steady-state window. Referencing the disturbance to its
own baseline rather than to the target keeps the fixed setpoint offset from
being counted as disturbance, so $\mathrm{RR} < 1$ means the controlled response
carries less energy than the disturbance it opposes. We also computed
$\mathrm{RR}$ on the open-loop trials of the same sessions, with the same window
and leak correction, as a paired comparator in which any error in the scale of
$D$ cancels between conditions. For that comparison the numerator was split into
the standing offset $(\bar{A}-r)^{2}$ and the fluctuation $\mathrm{var}(A)$,
which separates a response sitting at the wrong level from one that fails to
follow the disturbance.

\subsection*{Experimental Design and Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:stats}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\emph{Design.} Every closed-loop experiment is a within-session comparison:
open- and closed-loop trials, with the same reference and feedforward term,
were interleaved at random by the control software, so each session is its own
control. Sample sizes are 15 closed-loop sessions from 4
mice (2 male, 2 female; 1670 trials) for Figs.~\ref{fig:figure3}
and~\ref{fig:figure4}, four impulse-response sessions from three mice for
Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}, and three sinusoidal-tracking sessions from one mouse for
Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5} (Table~\ref{tab:cohort}). The unit of replication for
every between-condition claim is the session, and we state $n$ with its unit for
every test. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size; we
recorded as many sessions per animal as window clarity and opsin expression
allowed. Analyses were not blinded to condition; all analyses were automated
and applied identically to every condition.

\emph{Software and inference.} Analyses were performed in MATLAB R2025b. Mixed
models were fitted by restricted maximum likelihood. Fixed effects were
tested with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom, capped at
$n_{\mathrm{sessions}} - 1$ so that no test borrows trial-level degrees of
freedom; the cap can only make a test more conservative. Confidence intervals
are $t$-based on the same degrees of freedom. Significance was taken at
$\alpha = 0.05$, two-sided unless stated. We report each effect as an estimate
with its 95\% confidence interval, the test statistic with its degrees of
freedom, the exact $p$-value and $n$ with its unit; signed-rank tests are
reported with their $V$ statistic. Every test is listed in full in the
Statistical tables (Tables~\ref{tab:stat_counts}--\ref{tab:stat_fig4g}).
Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}G is descriptive, with its supporting tests in
Table~\ref{tab:stat_fig2g} (\nameref{sec:imp_state}). Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}
rests on three sessions in one mouse, too few for a session-level test, so it is
reported descriptively: the per-session direction of each effect and the pooled
mean $\pm$ SD, with no significance tests. The MPC replay
(\nameref{sec:mpc}) is a simulation and is reported descriptively.

\emph{Closed versus open loop} (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure3}). The primary test is on
one number per session and window, the ratio $r$ of the closed- to the open-loop
value (mean per-trial RMSE, or across-trial variance averaged over the window),
so that $r < 1$ means closed loop was better:
\begin{equation*}
    \log r \sim 1 + \left(1 \mid \mathrm{mouse}\right).
\end{equation*}
Its intercept is the log of the geometric-mean ratio, reported with its 95\%
confidence interval and as a percentage reduction, $100\,(1 - r)$. The random
mouse intercept treats the sessions of one mouse as correlated. For the full 0--3\,s window the mouse variance was
non-zero and the degrees of freedom collapsed toward the number of mice, so we
base inference on the transient and steady-state windows, which together span
it. As corroboration, the condition effect was also fitted to per-trial values,
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{RMSE} \sim \mathrm{condition}
    + \left(1 + \mathrm{condition} \mid \mathrm{session}\right)
    + \left(1 \mid \mathrm{mouse}\right),
\end{equation*}
so that each session contributes its own open-to-closed-loop gap. The same model
was applied to the log of each trial's mean squared deviation from its
condition's trial-averaged trace, the per-trial contribution to across-trial
variance. The comparisons are pre-specified, one per metric and window, and are
not corrected for multiple comparisons. Paired across-session comparisons used
the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, applied to the per-session log ratios
as a check on the mixed model.

\emph{State dependence} (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}D). For each state we fitted, to
steady-state RMSE,
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{RMSE} \sim \mathrm{condition} \times \mathrm{state}
    + (1 + \mathrm{condition} \mid \mathrm{session})
    + (\mathrm{state} - 1 \mid \mathrm{session})
    + (1 \mid \mathrm{mouse}),
\end{equation*}
with the state centered within session and scaled by its pooled standard
deviation, so every slope is in $\%\,\Delta F/F$ of RMSE per within-session
standard deviation of the state. The term $(\mathrm{state} - 1 \mid
\mathrm{session})$ gives each session its own uncorrelated state slope, so state
effects are tested against between-session variation; without it, the
false-positive rate in simulations of our design was 0.12--0.65 instead of
0.03--0.05 at $\alpha = 0.05$. This model tests two properties of each state.
\emph{Predictability} is the slope of open-loop error against state (open loop
as the reference level): a positive slope means the uncontrolled disturbance
grows with the state, so a fixed feedforward input anticipates the trial less
well. \emph{Regularizability} is the slope of closed-loop error against state
(the same model with closed loop as the reference level): a slope
indistinguishable from zero means the loop keeps the output regulated as the
state changes, and a positive slope means it loses it. The condition main
effect, the open-minus-closed-loop gap at mean state, is reported separately and
is neither property; the condition-by-state interaction (gap trend) is
listed in Table~\ref{tab:fig4_state_stats} but not interpreted, because the gap
can widen through a rising open loop as much as through a holding closed loop.
\emph{Attenuation} is the same model fitted to
$\log \mathrm{RMSE}$, whose condition-by-state interaction is the slope of
$\log(\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{CL}}/\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{OL}})$ against state:
how the fraction of open-loop error left by the loop, the empirical counterpart
of the sensitivity function, changes with state. As corroboration, per-session
ordinary least-squares slopes of RMSE on motion energy were summarized across
sessions with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (Table~\ref{tab:fig4_state_stats}).
The error decomposition of Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}C is descriptive and carries no
test.

\emph{Disturbance rejection} (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}G). We tested
$\log \mathrm{RR} \sim 1 + (1 \mid \mathrm{session})$, one-sided on the
intercept, and report the geometric mean of $\mathrm{RR}$ with its 95\%
confidence interval, corroborated by a one-sided signed-rank test on the
per-session median ratios. Tests of $\mathrm{RR} < 1$, and of closed- below
open-loop $\mathrm{RR}$, were one-sided because the hypothesis was directional
and fixed before the analysis. We
report the mouse-nested model, $\log \mathrm{RR} \sim 1 + (1 \mid \mathrm{mouse})
+ (1 \mid \mathrm{mouse}\!:\!\mathrm{session})$, alongside as the mouse-level
result; with three mice, one contributing a single session, its degrees of
freedom are ${\approx}1.2$ and the mouse and session variances cannot be
separated.

\emph{Multiple comparisons.} The state analysis of Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}D reads
two properties for each of four states, eight tests in all; attenuation is a
separate family of four. We report these $p$-values uncorrected and state the
size of each family; the Bonferroni thresholds are $0.05/8 = 0.0063$ and
$0.05/4 = 0.0125$, and Table~\ref{tab:fig4_state_stats} marks each effect that
clears its threshold. Each claim rests on one primary test; the corroborating
tests listed in the Statistical tables agreed with it.
