\section{Introduction}

The same stimulus delivered to cortex rarely evokes the same response twice. Evoked activity adds to, and is scaled by,
ongoing fluctuations present at the moment of stimulation~\citep{Arieli1996, Petersen2003, Goris2014}, and these
fluctuations are not noise: in awake mice they track movement and arousal~\citep{Niell2010, McGinley2015, Vinck2015,
musall2019single, Stringer2019, Salkoff2020}, alternate with slow, synchronized rhythms during quiet
wakefulness~\citep{Crochet2006, Poulet2008, Reimer2014}, and are shared across the two
hemispheres~\citep{Mohajerani2010, Shimaoka2019, Ye2026}. Direct perturbations inherit this dependence: cortical responses
to optogenetic and magnetic stimulation change with brain state~\citep{Mateo2011, Massimini2005}. Any fixed stimulation
protocol therefore delivers a different effective perturbation on every trial, which limits both the precision of
causal manipulations and their interpretation~\citep{Harris2011, Wolff2018}.

At the mesoscale, these state fluctuations are directly observable. Wide-field calcium imaging resolves population
activity across the dorsal cortex of behaving mice~\citep{allen2017global, makino2017transformation, musall2019single,
Peters2021}, and has been combined with spatially targeted optogenetic stimulation to map and silence cortical
areas~\citep{Lim2012, ZatkaHaas2021}. Recent preparations allow simultaneous imaging and stimulation anywhere on the
dorsal cortex of awake mice for months~\citep{Matveev2024}. These experiments have been open loop: the stimulus is fixed
in advance, and the state-dependent variability it encounters is averaged away after the fact.

Feedback offers a principled alternative. A controller that adjusts its input from a real-time measurement compensates
for disturbances it does not need to model~\citep{astrom1995pid}, so state-dependent variability becomes a disturbance
to be rejected rather than a confound to be averaged~\citep{grosenick2015closed, RittChing2015}. Closed-loop
optogenetic control has clamped the firing rates of single neurons to a target~\citep{newman2015optogenetic,
bolus2018design, bolus2021state}, shaped oscillations in rodents and primates~\citep{Siegle2014, nicholson2018analogue,
kanta2019closed, Zaaimi2022}, and, with two-photon imaging in the loop, targeted stimulation to the activity of
individual cells~\citep{Zhang2018}. Model-based approaches have identified input--output models of stimulation responses
across multiregional networks as a basis for controller design~\citep{Yang2018, Yang2021}. At the mesoscale, real-time
wide-field signals have so far been used to close the loop only through behaviour, as neurofeedback that animals
learn to modulate~\citep{clancy2014volitional, Neely2018, Gupta2026}.

Closed-loop optogenetic control of mesoscale cortical activity therefore remains, to our knowledge, untested, and two
questions determine whether it is feasible and what it can reveal. First, is the stimulation-to-activity relationship
simple enough to design a controller around? Population dynamics are often well captured by low-dimensional, linear
descriptions~\citep{Churchland2012, vyas2020computation}, and at the macroscale linear models outperform nonlinear ones,
because spatial and temporal averaging masks nonlinearity~\citep{Nozari2024}, but this has not been tested for
optogenetic drive of mesoscale signals. Second, which components of state-dependent variability can feedback actually
suppress? A controller can only correct what it measures and what its bandwidth allows, so the residual error under
feedback is itself informative about the structure of ongoing activity.

Here we establish closed-loop optogenetic control of mesoscale cortical activity in awake, head-fixed mice. We read out
regional $\Delta F/F$ in real time with wide-field calcium imaging and used it to drive a proportional--integral
controller, with feedforward compensation, that set the power of inhibitory optogenetic stimulation. Trial-averaged
suppression scaled approximately linearly with stimulation power and was well described by a low-order linear
model, whereas single-trial responses depended on pre-stimulus brain state. Across 15 sessions in 4 mice,
closed-loop control tracked a target activity level more accurately, and with lower trial-to-trial variability, than
open-loop stimulation. Feedback decoupled tracking error from movement-related state but not from slow (2--4\,Hz)
cortical rhythms, and partially rejected a disturbance shared with the contralateral hemisphere. Finally, adding a
preview of the reference demonstrated the feasibility of tracking time-varying targets. These results show that
approximately linear trial-averaged dynamics suffice to control regional cortical activity, and that closed-loop
perturbation can dissect which components of ongoing cortical variability are accessible to feedback.
